# Gamification mechanics and motor-skill learning in music practice (guitar focus)

Scope note: notes compiled 2026-11-03. Each finding is tagged where it matters as **[studied]** (peer-reviewed or thesis-level empirical result), **[company-reported]** (A/B results published by the company itself, not independently verified) or **[marketing]** (claims in product copy or commissioned surveys). Several primary PDFs (Sailer & Homner full text, Winstein & Schmidt 1990, Deci et al. 1999 full tables, the PUCP tablature thesis) could not be fetched; their numbers below come from abstracts, ERIC records or secondary summaries and are flagged.

## Which mechanics (streaks, stars/tiers, levels, unlocks, mastery meters, leaderboards, tempo ramps) have evidence of improving retention of a motor skill, and which mainly drive short-term engagement?

### Takeaway
The gamification literature shows small positive pooled effects on learning (g about 0.25 to 0.5) but almost none of it measures delayed retention of a motor skill, and the element-level evidence says points/levels/leaderboards raise output quantity, not intrinsic motivation or quality. The mechanics with real retention evidence are the ones that change *practice structure* (interleaving, spacing across days, error-targeted repetition, reduced-frequency feedback), which an app can wrap in mastery meters, tempo ramps and schedulers; streaks, stars and leaderboards are engagement/retention-of-users mechanics whose learning benefit is indirect (more sessions) and which can backfire.

### Cited Findings

Meta-analytic baseline for gamification in education:
- Sailer & Homner (2020, *Educational Psychology Review* 32:77-112) pooled random-effects Hedges' g: cognitive outcomes g = 0.49 [0.30, 0.69] (k = 19, N = 1,686); motivational g = 0.36 [0.18, 0.54] (k = 16, N = 2,246); behavioral g = 0.25 [0.04, 0.46] (k = 9, N = 951). Only the cognitive effect survived a sub-split to methodologically rigorous studies; motivational and behavioral effects were "less stable." Game fiction and social interaction moderated behavioral outcomes; competition *combined with collaboration* was the most effective social configuration. **[studied]** — [ERIC record EJ1245270](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1245270); [gamification-research.org summary](https://gamification-research.org/2019/08/does-gamification-of-learning-work/)
- A 2023 meta-analysis (41 studies, 49 samples, N > 5,071) reports g = 0.822 [0.567, 1.078] overall, but its moderators include an offline-environment estimate of g = 35.2 from a single study and a semester-plus duration estimate of g = 3.3, so its headline figure is inflated relative to the 0.40-0.60 range it attributes to Bai et al. 2020, Huang et al. 2020 and Sailer & Homner 2020. It does not analyse points, badges or leaderboards individually. **[studied, low rigor]** — [Frontiers in Psychology 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1253549/full)
- None of the meta-analyses above includes a music-performance or motor-retention outcome; their "cognitive" outcomes are test scores. — inference from the same two sources.

Element-level evidence (points, levels, leaderboards, badges):
- Mekler, Brühlmann, Tuch & Opwis (2017, *Computers in Human Behavior* 71:525-534; N = 273 online image-tagging task): points, levels and leaderboards each increased the *number* of tags produced versus control, but did not change intrinsic motivation, perceived competence or autonomy; the authors conclude they "functioned as extrinsic incentives, effective only for promoting performance quantity." **[studied]** — [Mekler et al. 2017 PDF](https://glassmanlab.seas.harvard.edu/annotated_works/mekler17.pdf); earlier 2013 CHI-PLAY study with the same pattern: [Mekler 2013](https://hcds.itu.dk/bibliography/mekler2013points)
- Hanus & Fox (2015, *Computers & Education* 80:152-161; two sections of a 16-week course, about 80 students): the gamified section (leaderboard + badges) showed *lower* intrinsic motivation, satisfaction, empowerment and final-exam scores, with intrinsic motivation mediating the exam effect; engagement with the mechanics declined across the term. **[studied]** — [review table summarising Hanus & Fox](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0042-5/tables/13)
- Novelty effect: Rodrigues et al. (2022; 756 students, full semester) found gamification's effect began to decline after about four weeks, with the decline lasting two to six weeks before partially recovering. Koivisto & Hamari (2014) found perceived enjoyment and usefulness of a gamified exercise app declined with length of use. Van Roy & Zaman (2018) found no novelty effect in a course with a long-term gamification design, so the effect is design-dependent. **[studied]** — [Rodrigues et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6); [Koivisto & Hamari 2014 summary](https://gamification-research.org/2014/03/demographics/); [Tsay et al. 2017 on term-start peaks](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/19946/7/19946%20TSAY_Student_Engagement_and_the_Novelty_Effect_2017.pdf)
- Guitar-specific: a University of Huddersfield MSc thesis (Grigutis, 2018) built a browser-based guitar tutor ("guitMaster") and ran three experiments with about 25 participants aged 20-35. Adding a leaderboard (personal competition, then group competition) increased usage time in the order control < personal < group, but "the confidence intervals on the usage time figure overlap," so the author declines to claim significance. It measured session time, not retention of playing skill. **[studied, thesis, inconclusive]** — [Grigutis 2018 thesis PDF](https://eprints.hud.ac.uk/id/eprint/34633/1/Laurynas%20Grigutis%20FINAL%20THESIS.PDF)

Streaks (Duolingo as cross-domain reference):
- Duolingo reports learners with a 7-day streak were 2.4x more likely to return the next day; decoupling the streak from the daily XP goal (one lesson now extends the streak) raised Day-14 retention +3.3% (relative), DAU +1%, learners on a streak after 20 days +10.5%, new learners on a streak +19%; a year later just over half of daily learners had a 7+ day streak, up from about a third. The post is explicit that the metric is *usage* ("we can't teach people if they stop using Duolingo"), not measured learning, and notes fewer learners hit their daily goals afterwards. **[company-reported]** — [Duolingo blog, "Improving the streak"](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/); [Duolingo blog, "How the streak builds habit"](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)
- Duolingo's retention PM states 600+ streak experiments; lessons: streaks "amplify, they don't replace value," keep the rule simple (tying streaks to XP confused users and was reverted), retention is most fragile in the first 7 days, "commit to my goal" copy and a user-chosen streak goal both increased retention. No claim is made about learning outcomes. **[company-reported, podcast]** — [Lenny's Newsletter episode page](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks); a third-party summary of the same talk reports the retention curve: the biggest jump is from a 1- to 2-day streak, gains shrink until day 7, then flatten but keep rising — [recall.it summary](https://www.recall.it/summary/lennys-podcast/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks-or-jackson-shuttleworth-group-pm-retention-team)
- Streak downside: a CHI 2016 qualitative study of the habit app Lift found streaks and reminders help people repeat behaviours but create *dependency*: when users abandon the app they often drop the behaviour too. **[studied]** — [Stawarz et al., "Don't kick the habit"](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/dont-kick-the-habit-the-role-of-dependency-in-habit-formation-app/); a Psychology Today column summarising *Journal of Consumer Research* experiments reports that people whose streak was broken were less likely to continue the habit, at least short-term — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation) (secondary; the primary paper was not retrieved)

Stars, tiers, levels and unlocks as used in music apps:
- Yousician: songs award points with a multiplier up to 5x for consecutive correct, in-time notes; points convert to silver/gold stars; stars gate progression along the learning path; missed stars can be replayed in the Workouts tab. The score reflects note and timing accuracy only. **[product documentation]** — [Yousician support: Practice vs Play mode](https://support.yousician.com/hc/en-us/articles/201558362-Practice-vs-Play-mode-Guitar-); reviewer caveat that score "says little about tone, dynamics, feel" — [Guitar World review](https://www.guitarworld.com/artists/yousician-fast-fun-way-learn-play-guitar-video)
- No controlled, peer-reviewed study of Yousician's learning outcomes was found; the closest is a four-player qualitative case study (Tan & Thiruvarul, 2021). A Swiss consumer test (Saldo, via test.de) judged the apps weaker for guitar than for piano. — [MMU case study](https://shdl.mmu.edu.my/12772/); [test.de](https://www.test.de/Musik-Lernapps-Kaum-gute-Noten-fuer-Handy-Musiklehrer-6221542-0/)
- Rocksmith's "fastest way to learn guitar" claim comes from a Ubisoft-commissioned Research Strategy Group in-home placement study (2013, "hundreds" of participants in four US metros) and the "95% improved" figure from a Toluna consumer survey; neither is peer-reviewed. **[marketing]** — [WARC campaign write-up](https://www.warc.com/content/article/rocksmith-2014-edition-60-day-challenge/en-gb/105783); [GodisaGeek news item](https://godisageek.com/2013/06/rocksmith-fastest-learn-guitar-research). The only academic evaluation found is a four-participant case study of the 60-day challenge. — [University of Sydney thesis](https://ses.library.usyd.edu.au/handle/2123/26621)

Practice-structure mechanics with retention evidence (what mastery meters and tempo ramps should encode):
- Error-targeted repetition beats volume: Duke, Simmons & Cash (2009, *JRME* 56(4):310-321; 17 advanced piano majors learning a 3-bar Shostakovich passage, retested next day). Next-day performance correlated with the percentage of error-free complete run-throughs (r = -.71 as reported, i.e. the rank scale ran inverted), percentage of correct attempts (r = -.51) and number of practice mistakes (r = .48); total time, number of repetitions and number of run-throughs did *not* predict. Correlational, next-day only. **[studied]** — [Structural Learning briefing on Duke et al. 2009](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/practise-the-hard-bit)
- Interleaving (contextual interference) in music: Carter & Grahn (2016; 10 advanced clarinetists, within-subjects, 12 min per piece either blocked or in alternating 3-min segments, blind-rated next day). Interleaved scores were numerically higher in every comparison; significant for one rater on the concerto excerpts (Wilcoxon p = .02) and technical studies (p = .02), marginal when averaged (p = .07). All 10 habitually practised blocked; 6 of 10 found interleaving more useful but 6 of 10 *preferred* blocked. Earlier: Stambaugh (2009) beginner clarinetists played faster at retention after interleaving; Stambaugh & Demorest (2010) no effect; Stambaugh (2011) effect for woodwinds, not brass. Sport analogue: Hall et al. (1994) baseball batting improved about 57% interleaved vs 25% blocked. **[studied, small samples]** — [Carter & Grahn 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full); [Stambaugh 2011 JRME](https://digitalcommons.georgiasouthern.edu/music-facpubs/2)
- Spacing across days: Shea, Lai, Black & Park (2000, *Human Movement Science* 19:737-760) and Lee & Genovese's (1988) review of 47 articles found distributed practice improves acquisition and retention of motor skills, with the benefit stronger for continuous than discrete skills. Caveats: one piano study found no spacing effect, and a 2025 serial-motor-skill study found short inter-trial intervals consolidated better. **[studied]** — [Frontiers 2017 review citing Shea et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00962/pdf); [Human Kinetics excerpt on continuous vs discrete](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/distribution-of-practice-in-motor-learning-and-development); [Donovan & Radosevich 1999 meta-analysis](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/1999-donovan.pdf); [massed practice for serial skills, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40778584/)
- Reduced-frequency feedback: Winstein & Schmidt (1990) and Winstein, Pohl & Lewthwaite (1994) found 100%-frequency knowledge of results or physical guidance produced the best acquisition but the poorest retention and transfer, versus faded/50% schedules (the guidance hypothesis). **[studied, lab tasks]** — [Winstein, Pohl & Lewthwaite 1994](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/effects-of-physical-guidance-and-knowledge-of-results-on-motor-le/); [Schmidt & Shapiro ARI report](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA191559.pdf). Contradiction: a 2016 study of *concurrent* auditory feedback on a complex task found the 100% group retained better than the 50% group and concluded "the guidance hypothesis was not supported," suggesting the effect depends on task complexity and modality. — [Frontiers in Neuroscience 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2016.00251/full)
- Autonomy as a learning variable: OPTIMAL theory (Wulf & Lewthwaite 2016) holds that autonomy support, enhanced expectancies and external focus each improve motor *retention*; Lemos et al. (2017) found children who chose their own demonstrations retained a ballet sequence better; a throwing-task replication found autonomy support improved retention while enhanced expectancies did not. **[studied]** — [Wulf & Lewthwaite 2016](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/kns_fac_articles/123); [autonomy support study](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/kns_fac_articles/191/); [Wulf & Lewthwaite 2021 Current Directions](https://gwulf.faculty.unlv.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Wulf_Lewthwaite_OPTIMAL_Current_Directions_2021.pdf)

### Inferences
- A "mastery meter" is only as good as what it meters. Yousician's stars reward consecutive in-time notes in a full run-through; Duke et al. suggest the predictor of next-day retention is the proportion of *error-free* attempts of the hard spot, so a meter fed by clean repetitions of the isolated failure point is better aligned with the evidence than a per-song star.
- Levels/unlocks and leaderboards are best read as volume multipliers (Mekler), useful only if the practice they multiply is well structured; they carry a documented risk of lowering intrinsic motivation (Hanus & Fox) and a 4-6 week novelty decay (Rodrigues).
- Streaks have the strongest *usage* evidence of any mechanic and no *learning* evidence; a one-small-action streak threshold (Duolingo's "one lesson") with a grace mechanism is the design the company converged on, and the dependency finding argues for pairing it with a visible practice-structure benefit so the habit survives the app.
- Interleaving and spacing produce a learning-performance dissociation: scores during the session look worse. Any adaptive system that reacts only to in-session accuracy will steer learners toward blocked, massed practice (what Carter & Grahn's participants preferred), so the scheduler should be allowed to impose interleaving and day-spacing rather than optimise session score.

### Gaps
- No randomised study was found that measures delayed retention of a guitar (or any instrument) skill as a function of streaks, stars, levels, badges or leaderboards; all music-app evidence is case studies, theses or company research.
- The primary Sailer & Homner PDF and the Winstein & Schmidt 1990 paper could not be fetched; figures are from ERIC/secondary summaries.
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of Yousician, Simply Guitar or Rocksmith+ learning outcomes exists in the sources found.

## How should difficulty adapt (slow after misses, speed up after accurate runs) to stay in the flow channel, and are there cited rules of thumb?

### Takeaway
Shipping systems adapt on accuracy alone: Yousician's auto-tempo moves in 5% steps between 25% and 125% of song speed, Rocksmith adds or removes notes per phrase across 20-30 levels, and Duolingo's Birdbrain targets per-learner difficulty. No cited rule like "slow 10% after two misses" exists in the literature; the closest quantified targets are the modelling result that about 85% training accuracy (15% error) maximises learning rate, and practitioner metronome increments of 2-5 BPM with a step back to the last clean tempo on failure.

### Cited Findings
- Yousician auto-adjust speed: "the app controls the song speed and adjusts it up or down depending on how you play," from 25% to 125% in 5% increments (documented for piano; guitar/bass/ukulele got "Auto" tempo via the pause menu in update 4.14, Aug 2020, with manual 5% steps). The trigger logic is not published. **[product documentation]** — [Yousician piano Practice/Play modes](https://support.yousician.com/hc/en-us/articles/204796991-Practice-and-Play-modes-in-piano); [Yousician update 4.14](https://yousician.com/blog/whats-new-in-yousician-update-4-14)
- Yousician on a wrong note in Practice mode: "the software pauses and waits for the user to get it right, then proceeds" (a wait mode), but "does not indicate what that note was." — [Grigutis 2018 thesis, section 2.6.5](https://eprints.hud.ac.uk/id/eprint/34633/1/Laurynas%20Grigutis%20FINAL%20THESIS.PDF)
- Rocksmith Dynamic Difficulty: songs are split into phrases; each phrase starts with few notes and more are added as the player "consistently completes" it; "if many notes are missed within a phrase, the game levels down." Each phrase has about 20 (reviewer) or 30 (Riff Repeater site) levels; level 5 is meant to be comparable across songs and levels 15-20 are typically technical solos; the Remastered edition added a Sightreading Level starting-point slider (up to 15 or MAX) and an "Override to Max" that disables DD per arrangement. Thresholds are not published anywhere found. **[product documentation / community]** — [Wikipedia: Rocksmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocksmith); [The Riff Repeater on Remastered DD settings](https://theriffrepeater.com/?p=12760); [CustomsForge thread on DD leveling rates](https://customsforge.com/topic/66780-dynamic-difficulty-changing-at-different-rates/)
- Rocksmith+ (2022): Adaptive Difficulty "adds or removes notes based on your accuracy"; an Adjustable Leveling Speed setting controls how fast notes are added when accurate; Note by Note mode in Riff Repeater advances only when the current note is played correctly. No thresholds or outcome data given. **[marketing]** — [Ubisoft News, Sept 2022](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/5XfINjoRmVJgBKR8fGt4EM)
- Reviewer and player criticism of DD: difficulty "spikes" when notes are added mid-song, the curve "wasn't smooth enough" for complex songs, and many players disable it in favour of Riff Repeater at reduced speed, then speed up; the original speed trainer "gradually speeds up as it loops." — [GameSpot review](https://gamespot.com/reviews/rocksmith-2014-edition-review/1900-6415495/); [Reddit progression thread](https://lr.vern.cc/r/rocksmith/comments/1cvzog6/whats_the_best_way_to_get_proper_progression_in/)
- Guitar-tutor thesis evidence on flow: in Grigutis' Experiment 1 "an unbalanced software difficulty level can lead to the learner suddenly receiving a low score due to a drastically increased difficulty which leads to discouragement by failure"; participants also quit the deliberately plain interface for boredom, i.e. both edges of the flow channel were observed. **[studied, thesis]** — [Grigutis 2018 thesis, ch. 7](https://eprints.hud.ac.uk/id/eprint/34633/1/Laurynas%20Grigutis%20FINAL%20THESIS.PDF)
- Duolingo Birdbrain: a model that estimates each learner's knowledge and each exercise's difficulty to predict whether the learner will get it right; lessons built at the learner's level "consistently helped our learners learn more" in A/B tests and raised next-day return; no target accuracy or effect sizes published; 20% of lessons personalised by Oct 2020. **[company-reported]** — [Duolingo blog: Introducing Birdbrain](https://blog.duolingo.com/learning-how-to-help-you-learn-introducing-birdbrain/)
- The 85% rule: Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen (2019, *Nature Communications* 10:4646) show for gradient-descent learners on binary tasks with Gaussian noise that learning rate is maximised at a training error of about 15.87% (about 85% accuracy); validated on neural-network and perceptual-learning models, and the author cautions it applies to simple tasks with a clear right/wrong answer. **[studied, modelling]** — [IDEAS record of Wilson et al. 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcom/v10y2019i1d10.1038_s41467-019-12552-4.html); [Neuroscience News summary](https://neurosciencenews.com/learning-optimized-15171/)
- Performance-based DDA in a 2D game (N = 221): the DDA group performed significantly better but showed no significant difference in enjoyment or flow, so performance-only adaptation does not automatically produce felt flow. **[studied]** — [Acta Ludologica 2022](https://actaludologica.com/performance-based-dynamic-difficulty-adjustment-and-player-experience-in-a-2d-digital-game-a-controlled-experiment/)
- Practitioner tempo-ramp conventions (not studies): raise the metronome only once a passage is under control, in steps of about 2 BPM (guitar exercises) to 5 BPM (studio teaching); when a step fails, drop back to the last clean tempo or a few BPM slower; some teachers alternate slower/faster tempos while trending upward. A forum poster recalls a study comparing incremental ramps with half-to-full tempo jumps but could not locate it, and neither could this search. **[practitioner advice]** — [Soundbrenner metronome exercises part 3](https://soundbrenner.com/blog/rhythm-timing-metronome-exercises-for-guitar-part-3); [Yamaha educator tips](https://hub.yamaha.com/music-educators/prof-dev/teaching-tips/practice-effectively/); [Living Pianos on slow practice](https://www.livingpianos.com/articles/is-slow-practice-important/); [Djangobooks forum thread](https://www.djangobooks.com/forum/discussion/18333)
- Feedback-timing evidence relevant to tempo ramps: in laboratory motor tasks, feedback on every trial speeds acquisition but hurts retention compared with faded or 50% schedules (see KQ1), while concurrent real-time feedback on a complex continuous task did not show that penalty. — [Schmidt & Shapiro ARI report](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA191559.pdf); [Frontiers in Neuroscience 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2016.00251/full)
- Music real-time feedback: in singing/violin intonation training, only participants who received real-time visual feedback learned and retained the pitch mapping at transfer, and real-time visual display is motivated as removing the delay of verbal KR; a percussion study found high-level real-time visual feedback improved imitation of expressive features but "may disrupt timing due to extra cognitive load." **[studied; exact paper titles not verified from the search snippets]** — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021 (intonation)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.684693/pdf); [Frontiers in Psychology 2019 (percussion)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00627/pdf)

### Inferences
- The two adaptation axes used by shipping apps are speed (Yousician: tempo percent) and density (Rocksmith: notes per phrase). Both react to accuracy only; neither published thresholds. A defensible synthetic rule from the evidence: aim for roughly 80-90% note accuracy in the current window (85% rule), step tempo by one 5% notch (Yousician's granularity, about 3-6 BPM at 60-120 BPM, matching the 2-5 BPM practitioner range) after a clean window, and on failure step *back to the last clean tempo* rather than stop, since Duke et al. tie retention to the proportion of clean attempts, not to total attempts.
- Make downward steps feel like a tool rather than a punishment (Rocksmith players disable DD when it spikes; OPTIMAL theory predicts autonomy support aids retention), e.g. offer the slow-down as a choice or explain it, and avoid mid-phrase density jumps.
- Reduce the salience of per-note feedback as accuracy rises (guidance hypothesis), but keep concurrent timing feedback when the task is continuous and complex, where the 2016 sonification result suggests it does not harm retention.

### Gaps
- No published accuracy thresholds, window sizes or hysteresis rules for Yousician auto-tempo or Rocksmith DD were found.
- No controlled study of adaptive tempo in instrumental practice, and no verified study comparing incremental tempo ramps to half-then-full tempo practice.
- The 85% rule has not been tested on multi-note motor sequences; treat it as a modelling prior.

## How do these apps handle the transition from scaffolded display (finger numbers, colours, labels) to unscaffolded play, i.e. fading scaffolds?

### Takeaway
The apps fade *task density and speed* (Rocksmith's per-phrase levels, Yousician's tempo) but fade *display scaffolds* only by user choice (Yousician's five notation views, colour-by-finger in piano); no app documented an automatic fade of finger numbers or colours. Learning science (guidance hypothesis, expertise-reversal effect) supports withdrawing such cues as accuracy rises because redundant guidance costs working memory and breeds dependency, but no study of fading visual cues on guitar was found.

### Cited Findings
- Yousician guitar offers five notation views the user switches between in the pause menu: tablature, tab & notes, notes, standard, standard & tab; piano offers enhanced, falling notes, coloured (thumb green, index yellow, middle purple, ring blue) and sheet. Switching is manual; no automatic progression is documented. **[product documentation]** — [Yousician: notation options in piano](https://support.yousician.com/hc/en-us/articles/208678605-Notation-options-in-piano); [Yousician: Practice vs Play mode (guitar)](https://support.yousician.com/hc/en-us/articles/201558362-Practice-vs-Play-mode-Guitar-)
- Yousician's note highway runs left to right with fret numbers on note blocks and chord names in colour, which the Huddersfield thesis notes "more closely resembles an actual guitar tablature layout," whereas Rocksmith's highway reads front-to-back, which "may present a difficulty when moving on from the game to reading music presented in guitar tablature format"; Rocksmith's arcade visuals are judged "slightly too far away from actual guitar practice." **[thesis, author's assessment]** — [Grigutis 2018 thesis, sections 2.6.3-2.6.6](https://eprints.hud.ac.uk/id/eprint/34633/1/Laurynas%20Grigutis%20FINAL%20THESIS.PDF)
- Rocksmith's scaffold is the arrangement itself: "most of the notes are intentionally left out" at first and added back as the learner hits notes, until the full arrangement is shown (see KQ2 for levels and the Override to Max escape hatch). — [Grigutis 2018 thesis](https://eprints.hud.ac.uk/id/eprint/34633/1/Laurynas%20Grigutis%20FINAL%20THESIS.PDF); [The Riff Repeater](https://theriffrepeater.com/?p=12760)
- Simply Guitar (JoyTunes, now Simply): an onboarding questionnaire sets a learning path (Guitar Basics, Guitar Essentials, then solo and rhythm courses); the app listens via the microphone and gives real-time note feedback; reviewers describe gamified early stages but flag detection problems; no documentation of display fading or speed control was found. Simply's claim of "proprietary AI" and a "research-backed curriculum" is company copy. **[reviews / marketing]** — [Guitar Chalk review](https://www.guitarchalk.com/simply-guitar-review/); [Guitar World review](https://www.guitarworld.com/reviews/simply-guitar-review); [CB Insights profile of JoyTunes](https://www.cbinsights.com/investor/joytunes)
- Fretboard-note trainers: Fret Pro uses spaced repetition with sessions split into short segments; FretMaster claims a spaced-repetition system that adjusts review timing by response speed; FredsBoard has Practice, Time Trial and Master modes and gives directional hints when the answer is one fret off; FaChords runs a 100-second game mode with a leaderboard and an easier mode with show-answer. These are app-store descriptions, not studies. **[marketing]** — [Fret Pro](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1519679458); [FretMaster](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id6757590159); [FredsBoard](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id6757344687); [FaChords trainer](https://www.fachords.com/fretboard-trainer-software/)
- Expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga 2007): instructional guidance that helps novices becomes redundant and harmful as task-specific knowledge grows, because learners "have to stop to integrate and cross-reference this redundant material"; the implication is "the need to adjust instructional methods and procedures as learners acquire more expertise." The "expertise" is narrow and task-specific. One replication found the reversal on transfer but not on retention. **[studied, cognitive tasks]** — [Kalyuga 2007 PDF](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)
- Guidance hypothesis applied to physical/visual guidance: high-frequency physical guidance produced the poorest retention of all conditions in Winstein, Pohl & Lewthwaite (1994). **[studied]** — [Winstein, Pohl & Lewthwaite 1994](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/effects-of-physical-guidance-and-knowledge-of-results-on-motor-le/)
- A NIME 2022 pilot of a multimodal music-learning system used an explicit "scaffolding then fading" curriculum, with two subjects over about 11 hours; qualitative only. — [NIME 2022 paper 39](https://nime.org/proc/nime22_39)
- Guitarists build visual representations of chord shapes that depend on playing experience (Crump et al. 2012), which suggests the visual scaffold is partly what is being learned, not just a prompt. — [Crump et al. 2012 PDF](https://www.crumplab.com/publications/Crump/files/4686/Crump%20et%20al.%20-%202012%20-%20Keeping%20an%20Eye%20on%20Guitar%20Skill%20Visual%20Representat.pdf)
- Transfer from plastic-controller games: a 2009 NCTU thesis found Guitar Hero play improved rhythm sense and pitch-interval variety on a real guitar regardless of prior experience, but players and forum reports agree the fingering does not transfer. **[thesis + anecdote]** — [NCTU thesis record](https://ir.lib.nycu.edu.tw/handle/11536/46413?locale=en)
- Fretboard sticker patents argue both ways: one lets the student drop a cue once a chord is mastered; an older one criticises removable markers for leaving no lasting reference. Inventor claims only. — [US 8704066](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8704066); [US 9613541](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9613541)

### Inferences
- Shipping apps have converged on letting the learner choose the notation level and on fading *content* (notes, tempo) automatically. Automatic fading of finger numbers and colours is unoccupied design space with theoretical backing (expertise reversal, guidance hypothesis) but no direct evidence.
- A fade schedule consistent with the evidence: keep full scaffolds while accuracy is below the target band, remove one cue layer (colour, then finger number, then fret label) when a passage is clean across spaced sessions rather than within one session (spacing and interleaving findings), and allow the learner to re-enable a cue (autonomy support), mirroring Rocksmith's per-arrangement override.
- Because the thesis found Rocksmith's front-to-back highway hinders transfer to tablature, a scaffold whose geometry matches the target reading format (left-to-right tab, or a fretboard that matches the physical instrument) should transfer better; this is an inference, not a tested result.

### Gaps
- No study of fading colour or finger-number cues on guitar, or of the right fade trigger (accuracy threshold, session count), was found.
- Simply Guitar's and Rocksmith+'s internal adaptation rules and any display-fading logic are undocumented.

## What does the research say about intrinsic vs extrinsic reward in music practice and over-justification effects?

### Takeaway
Expected tangible rewards reliably reduce free-choice intrinsic motivation in the general literature (d about -0.28 to -0.40 depending on contingency), the one music-course study of badges + leaderboard found lower intrinsic motivation and grades, and SDT studies of music students link autonomous motivation to more and better practice. Informational feedback that signals competence is the exception that SDT predicts can help, which is the design distinction between a reward and a progress signal.

### Cited Findings
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999, *Psychological Bulletin* 125(6); 128 studies): engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent tangible rewards reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation with d = -0.40, -0.36 and -0.28; unexpected rewards and verbal (informational) rewards did not undermine it; cognitive evaluation theory predicts the effect depends on whether the reward is expected and perceived as controlling. **[studied, meta-analysis]** — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 PDF](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf); the Cameron & Pierce dispute is summarised in [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
- The over-justification effect originates in Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (1973): children who expected a reward for drawing later showed less interest than those rewarded unexpectedly; anticipation, not receipt, drives the effect. — [SAGE Encyclopedia entry](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/socialpsychology/chpt/overjustification-effect)
- Hanus & Fox (2015): leaderboard + badges in a course lowered intrinsic motivation, satisfaction, empowerment and exam scores, with intrinsic motivation as mediator; the authors suggested the harm came from rewarding tasks students already found interesting. **[studied]** — [review table](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0042-5/tables/13)
- Mekler et al. (2017): points, levels and leaderboards did not *lower* intrinsic motivation either; the authors argue these elements "may afford feelings of competence and hence enhance intrinsic motivation" when perceived as informational rather than controlling. Commentators read the pair of studies as showing the elements are not harmful "as such," the framing is. **[studied]** — [Mekler et al. 2017](https://glassmanlab.seas.harvard.edu/annotated_works/mekler17.pdf); [gamification-research.org commentary](https://gamification-research.org/2014/08/gamification-absolved-2/)
- Evans (2015, *Musicae Scientiae* 19(1):65-83) proposes SDT as the unifying framework for music motivation: motivation becomes internalised to the extent that competence, autonomy and relatedness needs are met. **[conceptual review]** — [Evans 2015 PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/2015_Evans_SDT_MusicEdu.pdf)
- Evans & Bonneville-Roussy (2016, *Psychology of Music* 44(5):1095-1110): in university music students, need satisfaction and autonomous motivation were associated with more frequent practice, more frequent *quality* practice and a preference for challenging tasks. The 2024 follow-up (213 UK and Canadian students) supported the chain teacher autonomy support -> autonomous motivation -> practice time and practice quality. **[studied, self-report]** — [MetaTOC record of the 2016 paper](https://metatoc.com/papers/48746-self-determined-motivation-for-practice-in-university-music-students); [Bonneville-Roussy & Evans 2024 PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2024_Bonneville-RoussyEvans_MusicStudents.pdf)
- Feedback valence in a guitar tutor (Grigutis 2018): negative-framed feedback (red colour, deficit wording) drew direct complaints and the worst qualitative ratings; mixed feedback (negatives first, positives after) "appear[s] to be most effective though the data was not sufficient as the confidence intervals do overlap." **[studied, thesis, inconclusive]** — [Grigutis 2018 thesis](https://eprints.hud.ac.uk/id/eprint/34633/1/Laurynas%20Grigutis%20FINAL%20THESIS.PDF)
- OPTIMAL theory treats autonomy and positive competence expectancies as *learning* variables, not only motivational ones; autonomy support has the more consistent retention evidence. — [Wulf & Lewthwaite 2016](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/kns_fac_articles/123)
- Practitioner commentary (The Strad) argues rewards for practice undermine interest most when the task is inherently interesting or creative, and work "in the short term, but at great cost." **[opinion]** — [The Strad](https://thestrad.com/bribery-should-not-be-used-as-motivation-to-practise/1147.article)
- Duolingo's own retention lessons line up with the autonomy finding: a user-chosen streak goal and "commit to my goal" wording increased retention. **[company-reported]** — [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks)

### Inferences
- The SDT reading of the evidence: a star or meter that *informs* ("you played this passage clean 4 of 5 times at 80 BPM") supports competence; the same star presented as the *reason* to practise ("earn 3 stars to unlock") becomes a controlling, expected reward with the over-justification risk. Design the display so the metric describes the skill, not the payout.
- Leaderboards are the highest-risk element for music practice: solitary practice has no natural social-comparison frame, Hanus & Fox found harm, and Sailer & Homner found competition helped only when paired with collaboration.
- For a learner who already wants to play, the extrinsic layer should be thin and optional; for a learner at the start of habit formation, Duolingo-style streak scaffolding is defensible as a temporary externality, with the dependency finding arguing for fading it too.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found of tangible or point rewards on children's or adults' *instrument practice* specifically; the music evidence is SDT survey work plus the course-level Hanus & Fox study.
- The exact d for "all tangible rewards" and "expected tangible rewards" in Deci et al. 1999 was not confirmed from the fetched material.

## Any findings specific to tablature-first versus fretboard-diagram-first displays for guitar learners?

### Takeaway
Evidence is thin: one 2023 Peruvian thesis with 92 primary-school beginners found tablature improved performance over staff notation but not engagement; no study comparing tablature with fretboard diagrams (or with a note-highway) was found. Practitioner consensus is a hybrid (tab plus a rhythm staff), which is also the default of Hal Leonard, Guitar Pro and Songsterr, and Yousician lets the learner pick among tab, notes and standard views.

### Cited Findings
- PUCP thesis (2023; 92 first-time primary-school guitar students): "the hypothesis that tablature increases the performance of guitar students in contrast to its counterpart, sheet music, was fulfilled," while "the hypothesis that tablature ... increases student engagement was not fulfilled." Details of the design and statistics could not be fetched (site blocked). **[studied, thesis]** — [PUCP repository record](https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/index/handle/123456789/195859)
- Yousician supports tablature, tab & notes, notes, standard, and standard & tab for guitar, switchable per song; the app's highway shows fret numbers on note blocks and colour-named chords, left to right. — [Yousician notation options](https://support.yousician.com/hc/en-us/articles/208678605-Notation-options-in-piano); [Grigutis 2018 thesis section 2.6.5](https://eprints.hud.ac.uk/id/eprint/34633/1/Laurynas%20Grigutis%20FINAL%20THESIS.PDF)
- Rocksmith's front-to-back highway was judged a transfer obstacle to reading conventional tablature by the Huddersfield thesis author. **[thesis, assessment]** — [Grigutis 2018 thesis section 2.6.3](https://eprints.hud.ac.uk/id/eprint/34633/1/Laurynas%20Grigutis%20FINAL%20THESIS.PDF)
- Practitioner sources: plain tab omits rhythm, key and tempo; standard notation carries rhythm, harmony and dynamics but is slower to start; the common recommendation is a "tab plus rhythm" hybrid (six-line tab under a small standard staff), the default of Hal Leonard method books, Songsterr and Guitar Pro. The main source is a retailer blog that sells its own theory book. **[opinion]** — [Musiciangoods: tab vs standard notation](https://musiciangoods.com/en-gb/blogs/guitar/guitar-tab-vs-standard-notation); [Guitars on Main](https://guitarsonmain.com/blogs/news/learning-guitar-the-debate-between-tabs-and-standard-notation)
- Experienced guitarists hold visual chord-shape representations that scale with experience (Crump et al. 2012), which is the kind of knowledge a fretboard diagram addresses directly and tablature only implicitly. — [Crump et al. 2012](https://www.crumplab.com/publications/Crump/files/4686/Crump%20et%20al.%20-%202012%20-%20Keeping%20an%20Eye%20on%20Guitar%20Skill%20Visual%20Representat.pdf)
- A Saldo/test.de consumer test judged the music-learning apps weaker at teaching guitar than piano; no reason tied to notation was given. — [test.de](https://www.test.de/Musik-Lernapps-Kaum-gute-Noten-fuer-Handy-Musiklehrer-6221542-0/)

### Inferences
- The only controlled comparison favours tab over staff for beginner *performance*, consistent with tab's lower translation cost (string + fret given directly). Nothing tested supports a fretboard-diagram-first order; a diagram is the natural scaffold for chord shapes and note-name learning (Crump; the fretboard-trainer app genre), while tab is the natural scaffold for sequences in time. Treating them as complementary layers (diagram for *where*, tab for *when*) is consistent with the sources, not demonstrated by them.
- Because tab omits rhythm, a tab-first display should carry an explicit timing cue (Yousician's bouncing ball, a rhythm staff, or a metronome) or learners will practise pitches without timing, which is the skill the stated goal ("fretting notes in time") targets.

### Gaps
- No study was found comparing tablature with fretboard diagrams, note highways, or colour-coded notation on retention of fretting accuracy or timing.
- The PUCP thesis's effect sizes, group sizes and intervention length could not be retrieved.
