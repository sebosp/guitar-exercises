# Reinforcement and feedback schedules for real-time skill-training apps (guitar drill trainer)

Scope: how often, when and how to give (a) knowledge-of-results / knowledge-of-performance feedback (was the note right, was it in time) and (b) motivational reinforcement (streaks, celebrations, badges), with evidence from motor-learning research, product disclosures from Duolingo / Yousician / Simply / Rocksmith / Monkeytype / rhythm games, behavioural psychology on schedules, and UI guidance for live counters. Company-reported numbers are labelled as such throughout.

Vocabulary used below (motor-learning terms):
- KR = knowledge of results (outcome: hit/miss, early/late, score). KP = knowledge of performance (how the movement was done). Both are "augmented" feedback, as opposed to the learner's intrinsic feedback (hearing their own guitar, feeling the pick).
- Concurrent feedback = shown during the movement; terminal feedback = after it. Immediate vs delayed; 100% vs reduced relative frequency; faded = frequency decreases over practice; summary KR = one report after a block of N trials; bandwidth KR = feedback only when the error leaves a tolerance band.
- Acquisition performance (during practice with feedback) vs retention/transfer (later test without feedback). Retention is the measure of learning.

## Key question 1: What does motor-learning research say about feedback frequency? Does 100% immediate feedback on every note hurt retention compared with faded or summary feedback?

### Takeaway
The classic guidance-hypothesis literature (Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter 1984; Winstein & Schmidt 1990; Schmidt 1989 summary KR) found that 100% immediate KR speeds acquisition but reduced/faded/summary/bandwidth KR gives equal or better delayed retention, because learners stop processing their own intrinsic feedback when told the answer every trial. The effect is real but task-dependent: for complex, continuous tasks and for auditory feedback, several later studies find no penalty (or an advantage) for frequent feedback, and reviewers now say "too much" has no fixed threshold. The best-supported design is frequent feedback early, faded with skill, with feedback that highlights good trials.

### Cited Findings
- The guidance hypothesis: augmented information harms learning when it is given too frequently or in a form that is too easy to use; lowering KR frequency slows acquisition but improves retention; the proposed mechanism is that frequent, useful KR leads learners to ignore intrinsic (kinaesthetic) feedback — secondary descriptions of Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter (1984) in [a 1988 DTIC technical report on KR](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA191559.pdf) and [Marschall, Bund & Wiemeyer 2007](https://orbilu.uni.lu/bitstream/10993/4589/1/Publikation_Marschall_Bund_Wiemeyer_2007_EJournal%20Bewegung%20und%20Training.PDF).
- Winstein & Schmidt (1990), "Reduced frequency of knowledge of results enhances motor skill learning", J. Exp. Psychol. LMC 16:677–691: "Practice with reduced 50% KR improved skill retention over 100% KR", even though the 50% group performed worse during practice — as summarised by [Reid & Bolton Swafford 2020, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7205416). A Korean review describes the retention tests as 5 minutes and one day after acquisition — [koreascience](https://www.koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO199716549672161.pdf).
- Reid & Bolton Swafford's own rat study found 67% KR outperformed 100% KR in acquisition, and they state that human KR studies show acquisition improves with "but not too much" feedback and that "An understanding of what entails 'too much' feedback will require additional research" — [PMC7205416](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7205416).
- Summary KR (Schmidt et al. 1989, ballistic timing task, summary lengths 1/5/10/15 trials): longer summaries depressed acquisition performance but in a delayed no-KR retention test there was an inverse relation between summary length and absolute constant error; the authors favoured a guidance explanation: "relative to immediate KR, long KR summaries can provide detrimental effects in acquisition while enhancing retention performance" — [DTIC report](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA191559.pdf).
- Bandwidth KR (Sherwood 1988): quantitative error information is given only when performance is outside an established bandwidth; cited for producing higher movement consistency than no-bandwidth KR — [Sherwood 1988 record](https://vivo-cub.colorado.edu/display/pubid_105370); [UFMG bandwidth-KR paper](https://repositorio.ufmg.br/bitstream/1843/41055/2/Effects%20of%20bandwidth%20knowledge%20of%20results%20by%20components%20in%20learning%20a%20sequential%20motor%20task.pdf).
- Faded KR: a Brazilian study (Fonseca et al.) reports that a fading schedule, where KR frequency decreases over practice, seemed to give greater learning benefits than other distributions — [UFMG repository](https://repositorio.ufmg.br/handle/1843/49122).
- Learner-controlled KR: Patterson & Carter (2010) found learning advantages when learners chose which trials got KR, extending the guidance hypothesis "by showing learning advantages in a context of decreased KR frequency under direct control of the learner" — [Patterson & Carter 2010 PDF](https://raw.github.com/cartermaclab/carterlab-papers/main/2010_patterson-carter_learner-regulated-knowledge-of-results-during-the-acquisition-of-multiple-timing-goals.pdf).
- Feedback after good trials: in a beanbag-throwing task, all learners got KR on 3 of every 6 trials; the group that got KR on its three best trials showed no difference in practice but better delayed retention, interpreted as a motivational function of feedback (Chiviacowsky & Wulf 2007) — [sponet record](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4012854). Replicated in 28 children, with positive feedback also raising intrinsic motivation — [RBEFE 2011](https://www.scielo.br/j/rbefe/a/3wjqFcFJZ3rF8SqhLR3yjHj/); an earlier Brazilian child study of 40 found no retention difference — [RBEFE](https://revistas.usp.br/rbefe/en/article/view/16652).
- Self-controlled feedback: a 2014 replication of Chiviacowsky & Wulf (2005) found learners who decided after a trial whether to receive feedback were more accurate on delayed transfer than those who decided before, and that self-controlled schedules are "performance-dependent throughout the practice phase" (learners ask for feedback after trials they think were good) — [Frontiers in Psychology 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01325/full).
- Counter-evidence, auditory and complex task: Fujii, Lulic & Chen (2016), "More feedback is better than less", 20 adults learning a novel upper-limb coordination pattern with eyes closed, a 440 Hz tone whose intensity scaled with joint error (2 dB per degree), 100% vs 50% feedback over 100 trials, no-feedback retention immediately and next day: the 100% group had significantly smaller retention RMSE (F(1,18)=4.86, p<0.05, η²=0.21); no VE difference. Authors argue that guidance-hypothesis support (e.g. Winstein & Schmidt 1990) came from simpler tasks and visual feedback, that complex tasks may benefit from more frequent feedback, and that some auditory-feedback studies found no retention deterioration — [Frontiers in Neuroscience 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2016.00251/full).
- Sigrist, Rauter, Riener & Wolf (2013), "Augmented visual, auditory, haptic, and multimodal feedback in motor learning: a review", Psychon Bull Rev 20:21–53, frames the guidance hypothesis as "permanent feedback during acquisition leads to a dependency" and gives particular attention to task complexity and modality — [DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.3758/S13423-012-0333-8). Sonification work notes "no decline in performance was observed following the removal of augmented feedback in the auditory case, unlike the visual case" — [sonification of movement as concurrent feedback](https://scalda.mijn.bsl.nl/transposing-musical-skill-sonification-of-movement-as-concurrent/10207924).
- Music-specific: Maxfield (2011, Univ. of Iowa dissertation) applied low-frequency feedback theory to classical singing over a 15-week semester with an alternating single-subject design: 3 of 8 subjects retained more during high-frequency feedback phases, 5 retained less — mixed, small-n — [Iowa repository](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/doctoral/Application-of-principles-from-motor-learning-theory/9983776919202771).
- Attentional focus in musicians: Mornell & Wulf (2018) had skilled musicians play a piece under internal, external and control focus; the external focus ("playing for the audience and the expressive sound") produced higher expert ratings of expression and technical precision — [UNLV](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/kns_fac_articles/224).

### Inferences
- For a guitar drill with a metronome: per-note hit/miss and early/late is concurrent, terminal-per-note KR at 100% frequency. The classic literature predicts fast acquisition in-app and weaker carry-over to unassisted playing; the complexity/auditory counter-evidence (Fujii 2016; sonification work) says the penalty may be small or absent for a continuous auditory-motor task like playing to a click. The honest reading: the penalty is uncertain, so build in a cheap way to reduce dependence.
- Low-risk designs that match the evidence: (1) fade per-note markers as the learner's accuracy at a tempo rises (e.g. show early/late only when outside a tolerance band = bandwidth KR; tighten the band with skill); (2) keep a summary at the end of each bar/exercise (summary KR) even when per-note cues are hidden; (3) offer a "no cues" or "listen only" mode so the learner practises with intrinsic feedback and the app only reports at the end; (4) let the learner request detail after a run (self-controlled, after-trial); (5) make the end screen highlight the best bars/passages (feedback after good trials), not only errors.
- Bandwidth KR is a natural fit for timing: a note inside ±X ms is simply "in time" with no marker; only outliers get an early/late cue. Sherwood's consistency result suggests this also stabilises timing.
- Phrase per-note feedback in terms of the sound/outcome ("late", "muted") rather than the body ("move your finger faster"), per the external-focus results.

### Gaps
- I could not read the original Salmoni 1984 review, Winstein & Schmidt 1990 or Schmidt 1989 full texts; all details come from secondary descriptions. In particular I could not confirm the exact faded (100%→0%) schedule used in Winstein & Schmidt 1990.
- No study found that directly manipulates per-note feedback frequency for an instrument with a metronome; the nearest are singing (Maxfield 2011, mixed) and the auditory coordination task (Fujii 2016). The Sigrist 2013 rowing study ("Terminal feedback outperforms concurrent visual, auditory and haptic feedback in learning a complex rowing-type task") was mentioned in results but not retrieved.
- No evidence found on whether a visual per-note display during playing harms performance by pulling attention (dual-task / distraction); the attentional-focus literature is about instructions, not displays.

## Key question 2: What reinforcement schedules do Duolingo, Yousician, Simply Guitar, Rocksmith, Monkeytype and rhythm games use at per-note, per-exercise, per-session and per-week levels, and what do they show at the end of an exercise?

### Takeaway
All of the music/typing/rhythm apps give 100% immediate per-note KR (perfect/early/late/miss) during play, a terminal summary (score, stars, accuracy %, WPM/raw/consistency) at the end, and a daily streak plus weekly goal/league layer on top. Rhythm games add a combo multiplier that resets on a miss. Only Duolingo publishes quantitative experiment results; the music apps' schedules are known only from reviews, support pages and a patent.

### Cited Findings
Duolingo (company-reported A/B results):
- Streak originally required meeting the daily goal; Duolingo separated them so that one lesson extends the streak. Learners with a 7-day streak were 2.4x more likely to use the app the next day than learners without one; the change raised learners on a 7+ day streak by over 40%; A/B relative lifts: day-14 retention +3.3%, DAU +1%, daily learners on a streak after 20 days +10.5%, new learners on a streak +19%; trade-off: fewer learners reached their daily goals. A year later "just over half of our daily learners have a streak at least 7 days long", up from about a third — [Duolingo blog, Improving the streak (2020)](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak).
- Streak Wager (spend in-game currency to commit to 7 more days, paid back double on success): significant Day-1/7/14 retention gains, "Day-7 retention showing the greatest improvement at +14%". Weekend Amulet (store item that keeps the streak over a weekend): offered learners "4% more likely to come back a week later" and 5% less likely to lose their streak; weekend DAU normally drops 5–10%. "learners who binge on Duolingo lessons were much more likely to abandon the app" than those who pace themselves — [Duolingo blog, How streaks keep learners committed (2017/2020)](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/).
- Allowing two Streak Freezes at once "increased the relative number of active learners on Duolingo every day by +0.38%" and did not push people to take more days off — [Duolingo blog, How the streak builds habit](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit). Outages are repaired by applying the freeze logic to the affected day — [Duolingo blog, Protecting streaks from site issues](https://blog.duolingo.com/protecting-streaks-from-site-issues).
- Growth model (ex-CPO Jorge Mazal): CURR (active today and at least once in prior 6 days) had 5x the DAU impact of the next metric; leaderboards raised learning time 17% and tripled highly engaged learners; a "gamified moves counter" was "completely neutral"; the streak-saver notification was the "first big win" (no figure); notification volume was gated by a "protect the channel" rule needing CEO approval; share of DAU with a 7+ day streak rose almost 3x to more than half of DAU — [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth).
- Per-lesson layer: Duolingo's own posts cover streak and goals; the per-answer feedback (green/red, combo bonus XP) and lesson-end XP screen were not described in the sources retrieved (see Gaps).

Yousician (third-party descriptions and a patent):
- Practice mode gives instant feedback on each note with labels like "perfect" or "late/early"; the end of each session shows a score and a number of stars — [StriveCloud gamification playbook](https://www.strivecloud.io/play/yousician-gamification-playbook). Gold stars require playing with no mistakes; more accuracy = more points; critics note a high score "means you hit the notes in time" and says little about tone or feel, so star-chasers can become "technically accurate but stiff" — [Violet Recording review](https://violetrecording.com/yousician-review/).
- Session goals can be set as "play for stars or minutes"; the Workouts tab is for collecting missing silver and gold stars — [Yousician support](https://support.yousician.com/hc/en-us/articles/201558312-How-to-navigate-through-the-main-tabs).
- A patent (US9218748B2, "System and method for providing exercise in playing a music instrument") describes evaluation data on separate items (note, interval, chord) shown "substantially online during the playing of the exercise" — [Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9218748) (not confirmed as Yousician's implementation).

Simply Guitar / Simply Piano (marketing and reviews):
- The recognition engine checks each note via microphone or MIDI ("Simply Piano listens to every note you play") and gives real-time feedback — [hellosimply](https://welcome.hellosimply.com/geist_learn_piano). Progress bars fill from incomplete to complete and the app shows on-screen celebrations — [Guitar Chalk review](https://www.guitarchalk.com/simply-guitar-review/). No source found describing a star system (see Gaps).

Rocksmith (Ubisoft + reviews):
- Points per note hit, impossible to fail a song; dynamic difficulty adds or removes notes per phrase based on accuracy ("Adaptive Difficulty adds or removes notes in real time based on your accuracy") — [Ubisoft accessibility spotlight](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/5XfINjoRmVJgBKR8fGt4EM/rocksmith-accessibility-spotlight). Riff Repeater: play a section cleanly to step difficulty up; "leveler" gives five attempts to play a riff correctly to raise its difficulty in the song — [GamingTrend review](https://gamingtrend.com/feature/reviews/can-always-get-better-rocksmith-2014-experienced-review). Rocksmith+ mastery = difficulty x accuracy (50% difficulty at 90% accuracy = mastery 45) — [Ubisoft dev diary Sept 2021](https://www.ubisoft.com/en-ca/game/rocksmith/plus/news-updates/2rN7QW1RC4E4NsipNX1cz7/rocksmith-dev-diary-september-2021). Players complain that difficulty changes mid-song unpredictably — [CustomsForge thread](https://customsforge.com/topic/66780-dynamic-difficulty-changing-at-different-rates/).

Monkeytype (official about page):
- After a test: "you will be able to see your wpm, raw wpm, accuracy, character stats, test length, leaderboards info and test info", with floating-point values on hover; consistency = coefficient of variation of raw WPM scaled 0–100, "Closer to 100% is better"; plus a WPM-over-time graph and per-character breakdown — [monkeytype.com/about](https://monkeytype.com/about). No per-keystroke celebration, only a running WPM/timer by default; the whole reward is the end screen.

Rhythm games (wikis and devlogs):
- Tiered timing windows with per-tier points: one blueprint uses ±25/50/100/150 ms for Perfect/Great/Good/Bad worth 100/75/50/25 points — [Godot rhythm genre skill](https://skills.cat/skills/thedivergentai/gd-agentic-skills/godot-genre-rhythm); another uses 60/120/180 ms worth 300/200/100 — [dev.to](https://dev.to/skywalker_2de7de5f97df567/building-fair-rhythm-game-judgement-windows-in-javascript-1og9); osu! judgement reference — [osu! wiki](https://osu.ppy.sh/wiki/en/Gameplay/Judgement/osu%21).
- Combo multiplier steps at thresholds (Rizline: x1 below 5, up to x4 at ≥20; a miss or Bad resets to 1) — [Rizline scoring](https://rgwiki.stary.pc.pl/wiki/Rizline:Max_score); Rhythia: Perfect hits feed the multiplier, Great/Good give diminishing returns — [Rhythia mechanics](https://shapes.inc/fandom/rhythia/gameplay-mechanics). Windows are scaled with playback rate so timing feels consistent at different speeds — [fluXis scoring](https://mintlify.wiki/InventiveRhythm/fluXis/gameplay/scoring).

Streaks elsewhere:
- Newsreel (gamified news app): 277 Penn State students required to do a 20-day streak for course credit; 47% completed it — confounded with grading — [Press Gazette](https://pressgazette.co.uk/news/streak-mechanism-key-gen-z-gamified-app-newsreel/).

### Inferences
- The industry layering is consistent: per-note = instant judgement (no celebration beyond a colour/label), per-exercise = score + stars/accuracy % + best-ever comparison, per-day = streak, per-week = league/goal. Monkeytype shows that a skill tool can run with almost no in-run reinforcement and a rich end screen and still retain a competitive community.
- Rocksmith's "difficulty x accuracy" mastery is a useful model for a drill trainer: report mastery as tempo-fraction x accuracy so that a clean run at 60% speed is not confused with a sloppy run at full speed.
- Rhythm-game combos are the per-note equivalent of a variable-ratio/avoidance schedule: they produce intensity but also "one slip wipes it" frustration; Rhythia's variant (only Perfects feed the multiplier, Goods still count) is gentler.
- Duolingo's "gamified moves counter: completely neutral" is a warning that adding another live counter does not by itself move retention.

### Gaps
- No primary Yousician or Simply design write-ups exist publicly; their star thresholds, scoring formulas and lesson-end screens are known only from reviews. Simply's star/score system was not confirmed at all.
- Duolingo's per-answer feedback (combo bonus, "You're on a roll") and lesson-end XP screen were not covered by the retrieved posts; the Lazyweb summaries of a streak-goal screen that "was never stored" yet improved retention are unsourced and were not relied on.
- No official Rocksmith threshold for the accuracy that steps difficulty up or down.
- The claim that Duolingo ran 600+ experiments on the streak (Medium) is unverified.

## Key question 3: Streaks — what evidence exists on their effect on retention and on burnout/anxiety; what mitigations are used?

### Takeaway
Causal evidence that streaks raise short-term retention comes almost entirely from Duolingo's own A/B tests; peer-reviewed work (Silverman et al. 2023, JCR; Etkin 2016; a CHI 2016 habit-app study) shows the downside: breaking a streak makes users more likely to quit entirely, knowing a repair exists lowers motivation to keep the streak, quantification drains enjoyment, and habit-app users become dependent on the app. Habit research (Lally 2010) says a missed day does not harm habit formation, which argues for forgiving streaks. Mitigations in use: streak freezes (Duolingo: two at once, +0.38% DAU), weekend protection, paid/free repair within 48 h, counting one short action as enough, avoiding guilt messaging.

### Cited Findings
- Retention effects (company-reported): see Duolingo numbers in Q2 — 7-day streak holders 2.4x more likely to return next day; streak wager +14% day-7 retention; weekend amulet +4% week-later return — [Improving the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak); [How streaks keep learners committed](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/). Duolingo's growth lead cautions that the early "10-day streak reduces drop-off" finding was "a lot of this was simply correlation and selection bias" — [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth).
- Loss aversion as the mechanism: Duolingo says "Many learners are proud of their streak and highly averse to losing it" and "losing a streak can be very discouraging" — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/).
- Silverman, Barasch, Inman, Wood & Lee (2023), "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions", Journal of Consumer Research 49(6):1095–1117: users often treat extending the streak as more important than the activity; users who break a streak are more likely to stop using the platform entirely; users are less motivated to keep streaks when they know they can repair them — as summarised by [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification).
- Etkin (2016), "The Hidden Cost of Personal Quantification", JCR 42(6):967–984: people do more of what they track, but quantification can drain enjoyment so tracked activities feel like work ("streak creep") — via [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification).
- CHI 2016 late-breaking work (UCL) on the habit app Lift: reminders and streaks support repetition "but at the same time create a dependency: on-going app use is often required to achieve lasting change"; participants who abandoned the app often disengaged from the behaviour — [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1477627/1/Chi%202016%20LBW%202.1%20camera%20ready.pdf).
- Habit formation: Lally et al. (2010), 96 participants, 84 days: median 66 days to peak automaticity (range 18 to a predicted 254), and "missing one opportunity to perform the behavior did not materially affect the habit formation process" — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit).
- Pacing: "learners who binge on Duolingo lessons were much more likely to abandon the app"; giving the option to take a break makes learners "more likely to do more in the long run" — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/).
- Mitigations in use: two equipped Streak Freezes (+0.38% DAU, no increase in days off) — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit); weekend amulet — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/); minimal action (one lesson) extends the streak — [Improving the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak); third-party guides report streak repair within 48 h (free for Plus) — [Duolingo Guides](https://duolingoguides.com/duolingo-streak-repair-not-working/) (unconfirmed on Duolingo's own pages); a June 2026 limited-time "revive broken streak" event — [Android Authority](https://androidauthority.com/duolingo-revive-broken-streak-event-3673004/).
- Design critiques: The Decision Lab recommends tying streaks to activities users already value, restraint (not a counter on every feature) and avoiding guilt-based messaging, citing Duolingo's "You made Duo sad" notifications — [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification). A UX Collective piece proposes healthier framings (weekly rather than daily streaks, "days practised" totals) — [uxdesign.cc](https://uxdesign.cc/3-reframing-streaks-on-duolingo-5-ideas-for-a-more-healthy-and-flexible-approach-to-language-8fd89545771e) (opinion). A qualitative case study of gamification misuse in a language app exists — [arXiv 2203.16175](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.16175) (not read).

### Inferences
- For a practice tool whose goal is skill (not DAU), the evidence favours a forgiving streak: count any short drill as a practised day; give automatic freezes (one or two in reserve, auto-applied); or count "days practised this week/month" alongside the streak so that a break does not zero the visible record. Lally's result means a one-day miss costs nothing in habit terms, so the UI should not punish it.
- Silverman's finding that known repairs lower motivation argues for making freezes automatic and quiet rather than a purchasable "undo" the learner plans around.
- Avoid guilt notifications; if reminders exist, frame them on the learner's own goal (tempo target) rather than the streak count.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that isolates streak effects on retention across apps; the Silverman et al. paper's experiments and numbers were not read directly (only the Decision Lab summary). The medRxiv run-streaking preprint (21 recreational runners who broke a long streak) returned 403 and its findings are unknown. A "2021 Computers in Human Behavior" study on streak anxiety cited by blogs could not be located and should be treated as unverified. The "nearly 60% give up after breaking a streak" figure is unsourced marketing.

## Key question 4: How should an app celebrate success proportionally so that celebrations do not become noise?

### Takeaway
Design guidance (not controlled research) converges on: match celebration intensity and length to the rarity and value of the event, attenuate repeated celebrations, keep micro-feedback at 200–500 ms and barely noticeable, honour reduced-motion, and spend "juice" on moment-to-moment feedback while reserving big moments for genuine milestones. Motor-learning evidence adds a reason to emphasise good trials: feedback after good trials improves retention and intrinsic motivation. Behavioural evidence adds a warning: withdrawing reward once given is aversive, so a celebration ladder must be stable.

### Cited Findings
- "Juice it or lose it" (Jonasson & Purho, GDC Europe 2012) showed a dull game made exciting without changing rules; Folmer Kelly's counter-talk argued over-polish can cost immersion — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/has-your-game-got-juice-more-). "Throwing juice at everything" overwhelms players; use it selectively — [Wayline](https://www.wayline.io/blog/the-seductive-squeeze-when-juice-in-game-development-becomes-a-crutch). One-off moments gain little from juice; "Better to juice the moment to moment events" — [itch.io devlog](https://itch.io/post/734267).
- Microinteraction guidance: durations of 200–500 ms; "users should barely notice a microinteraction unless they're looking for it"; excessive or prolonged animations become annoying "especially for repetitive actions"; A/B test to calibrate — [Cieden](https://cieden.com/book/sub-atomic/microinteractions/what-are-microinteractions), [Cieden, designing neat microinteractions](https://cieden.com/book/sub-atomic/microinteractions/designing-neat-microinteractions); too much animation moves from delight to annoyance — [Lordicon](https://lordicon.com/blog/from-delight-to-annoyance-when-too-much-animation-hurts-ux). Confetti only for high-value milestones, never routine actions; attenuate (full effect the first few times, then a subtle checkmark); respect prefers-reduced-motion — [harness-engineering micro-interactions guide](https://openskillindex.com/skills/intense-visions-harness-engineering-design-micro-interactions-9eef1d) (practitioner guidance, not studied).
- Feedback after good trials improves delayed retention and intrinsic motivation (Chiviacowsky & Wulf 2007; 2011 child replication) — [sponet](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4012854), [RBEFE 2011](https://www.scielo.br/j/rbefe/a/3wjqFcFJZ3rF8SqhLR3yjHj/).
- Hopson, "Behavioral Game Design": "reducing the level of reinforcement is a very punishing thing for your players"; "Sudden loss of reward is very aversive and should be avoided when possible"; "Violation of expectations is perceived as an aggressive act" — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behavioral-game-design).
- Simply Piano uses on-screen celebrations and progress bars that fill as the learner advances — [Guitar Chalk](https://www.guitarchalk.com/simply-guitar-review/); Yousician shows stars and a score only at the end — [StriveCloud](https://www.strivecloud.io/play/yousician-gamification-playbook); Monkeytype gives no in-run celebration and a data-rich end screen — [monkeytype.com/about](https://monkeytype.com/about).

### Inferences
- A proportional ladder for the guitar trainer: per note = a colour/tick change only (no sound, no motion beyond a 100–200 ms highlight), and consider hiding it inside the tolerance band (bandwidth KR); per bar or phrase = a small, silent "clean bar" marker; per exercise cleared at a tempo = the one real moment (short animation, the number that improved, best-ever comparison); per tempo milestone or first clean run at target tempo = the largest, rarest celebration; per day = streak tick on the end screen, not a separate interruption.
- Attenuate: the first clean exercise gets the full effect, repeats get a quieter version; never remove a tier the learner has already seen (Hopson's extinction warning), only shrink it.
- The end screen should lead with what went well (best bar, longest in-time run) before the error list.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on celebration intensity vs. retention or on confetti attenuation; all of this is practitioner guidance. Schell's Art of Game Design was not retrieved; the Jonasson/Purho talk transcript was not found.

## Key question 5: How should counters and live stats be shown during an exercise without distracting?

### Takeaway
Direct research on live-stat displays during music performance was not found. The practical rules come from typography/UI (tabular figures to stop width jitter, fixed positions), from the attentional-focus literature (keep the learner's attention on the sound, not on themselves), from rhythm-game convention (judgement near the note lane, score/combo in a fixed peripheral corner, hidden multipliers), and from Monkeytype's minimal in-run display with the rich data moved to the end.

### Cited Findings
- `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums` (OpenType `tnum`) renders digits at equal width and removed counter jitter in a 0-to-5 demo; not all fonts ship tabular glyphs — [Go Make Things](https://gomakethings.com/preventing-layout-shift-with-numbers-using-css/); recommended "for aligning tabular data and preventing layout shifts of dynamically-changing content", e.g. a countdown timer — [Dialpad Dialtone](https://dialtone.dialpad.com/utilities/typography/font-variant-numeric.html); Tailwind `tabular-nums` maps to `tnum` — [Tailwind](https://v2.tailwindcss.com/docs/font-variant-numeric). Many Google Fonts variants lack the feature; self-hosting restores it — [Dr. Web](https://www.drweb.de/css-ziffern-font-variant-numeric/).
- Rizline keeps its combo multiplier hidden and only exposes combo count — [Rizline scoring](https://rgwiki.stary.pc.pl/wiki/Rizline:Max_score); Monkeytype's default in-run UI shows a live WPM/timer and moves everything else to the results screen — [monkeytype.com/about](https://monkeytype.com/about).
- External focus (on the sound, on the audience) beats internal focus for expression and technical precision in skilled musicians — [Mornell & Wulf 2018](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/kns_fac_articles/224); feedback about movement effects beats feedback about body movements (Wulf & Lewthwaite tennis) — same source family via [Sage review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356221101431).
- Duolingo's gamified moves counter was "completely neutral" for retention — [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth).

### Inferences
- Live counters (notes, correct, in time, NPM): render with tabular numerals in a font that has them (Barlow Condensed, used by the app, should be checked for `tnum`; otherwise use a monospace/tabular face for digits), fix the widest expected width (e.g. reserve 3–4 digit cells), never reflow neighbours, no colour flashes on increment. Place the block in one peripheral corner at constant position; put the only moving element (the current note/beat cursor) where the eye already is.
- Show at most one derived live number (e.g. in-time %) and keep the raw counts for the end screen; NPM changes every beat and is the most distracting, so either damp it (rolling average) or hide it until the end.
- Per-note judgement belongs at the note position (like rhythm-game judgements under the lane), not in the counter block, so the learner never has to look away from the notation to know a note was late.

### Gaps
- No study found on glanceable HUD placement for musicians or on whether live accuracy percentages hurt performance (choking / self-focus literature only addresses instructions). Peripheral-vision HUD research from aviation/gaming was not searched.

## Key question 6: Schedule type — fixed vs variable ratio — and the ethics of variable reward

### Takeaway
Hopson's primer (and the Skinnerian literature it summarises) says variable-ratio schedules produce the steadiest, highest response rates, fixed-ratio schedules produce post-reward pauses that grow with ratio size, and removing or thinning reward is experienced as aggression. Critics (Harris, Center for Humane Technology; critics of Eyal's Hooked) argue that deliberately variable rewards are the slot-machine mechanism and should be made predictable. For a skill trainer the informative reward (your timing improved) is intrinsically variable because performance varies; the design question is whether to add artificial unpredictability on top, and the ethics literature says not to.

### Cited Findings
- Hopson, "Behavioral Game Design" (Gamasutra 2001): fixed ratio = reward after a set number of actions (extra life after 20 kills), players pause then burst; variable ratio = number varies around an average, "steady, high activity"; fixed interval = first response after set time, activity rises as the time nears; variable interval = steady but slower activity. Post-reward pause grows with ratio size and rising requirements can make it "endless" so the player "walks away". Extinction: ratio-schedule players keep working a while, then frustration, which "is irrational, but real nonetheless". Avoidance schedules (prevent a loss, e.g. house decay in Ultima Online) sustain "behavioral momentum" cheaply — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behavioral-game-design).
- Harris: "if you want to maximise addictiveness, all tech designers need to do is link a user's action (like pulling a lever) with a variable reward"; addictiveness is maximised when the rate of reward is most variable; proposed fix: "converting intermittent variable rewards into less addictive, more predictable ones" — [Mobile Ecosystem Forum summary of Harris's essay](https://mobileecosystemforum.com/2017/04/27/slot-machines-in-our-pockets-are-apps-too-good-for-our-own-good/); UK Parliament oral evidence, "every time you scroll, you might as well be playing a slot machine" — [committees.parliament.uk](https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/8000/html).
- Eyal's Hooked loop is trigger → action → variable reward → investment; critics object that it recommends operant conditioning to create addiction; Eyal has said 1–5% of users struggle to stop, and offers a "Manipulation Matrix" (be a facilitator, not a dealer) — [SD Times](https://sdtimes.com/addiction/ethics-addiction-and-dark-patterns/).
- Streaks are an avoidance schedule by Hopson's definition (act to avoid losing the chain), and Silverman et al. show the loss is followed by quitting — [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification).
- Rhythm-game combo multipliers are tiered fixed-ratio schedules with reset-on-miss — [Rizline](https://rgwiki.stary.pc.pl/wiki/Rizline:Max_score).

### Inferences
- For the drill trainer, keep rewards predictable and earned: stars/tempo badges at known thresholds (fixed ratio with transparent criteria), no loot-style random bonuses, no surprise multipliers. The natural variability of one's own accuracy already supplies the "will I get it this time" tension without manipulation.
- Avoid growing ratio requirements that produce long post-reward pauses (e.g. needing 10 clean runs for the next star); instead step tempo in small increments so the next attainable reward is always close.
- If a streak is kept, treat it as the avoidance schedule it is and soften the loss (freezes, weekly framing) rather than exploit it.

### Gaps
- Harris's original Medium essay was not fetched directly; quotes come from summaries and the parliamentary transcript. No quantitative study found comparing fixed vs variable reward schedules on learning outcomes in educational apps.
