# Meter mastery, fade feedback, soften the miss

Gamification helps a learner internalize a motor skill only indirectly: the mechanics with real retention evidence are the ones that change *how* practice is structured (error-targeted repetition, interleaving, spacing across days, faded feedback, learner choice), while points, levels, badges and leaderboards raise the *quantity* of practice without improving its quality and can lower intrinsic motivation. Feedback on every note speeds in-session acquisition but, in the classic motor-learning literature, weakens retention; the schedules that survive a delayed test are faded, bandwidth (only outside a tolerance), summary (per phrase or run), self-requested, and feedback that highlights the learner's *best* trials. Streaks have the strongest usage evidence of any mechanic (Duolingo: a 7-day streak makes next-day return 2.4x more likely) and no learning evidence, and broken streaks drive quitting, so they should be forgiving and quiet. For a long session in a dim room, luminance rather than hue decides comfort: a cream light page and a warm near-black dark surface, text held to APCA levels rather than only WCAG ratios, and state colors chosen so they differ in lightness and on the blue-yellow axis (orange against blue, never green against red) because 8% of men cannot separate the traffic-light pair. A miss should be reported where it happened, with its direction ("early", "flat"), in a desaturated warm color with a shape cue, for a few hundred milliseconds, never with a flash, a shake or a global verdict; an automatic tempo drop belongs in a 3-5 s transient chip ("Tempo 80 to 72", action "Keep 80"), ideally forewarned, because Duolingo found that warning learners about a hard item made mistakes sting less. The evidence base is uneven: the structure-of-practice and feedback-frequency findings come from controlled studies, the color and error-UI advice rests on official guidelines plus simulation, and nearly everything about the music apps themselves (Yousician, Rocksmith, Simply) is product documentation or reviews, not trials.

## Points raise output, practice structure raises retention

Across education research, gamification earns a small-to-moderate pooled effect, and that effect says nothing about motor skill. Sailer and Homner's meta-analysis found **cognitive g = 0.49, motivational g = 0.36 and behavioral g = 0.25**, with only the cognitive effect surviving a restriction to rigorous studies, and competition helped only when combined with collaboration ([ERIC record EJ1245270](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1245270)). None of the meta-analyses includes a music-performance or delayed-retention outcome. At the element level the picture is sharper and less flattering: in Mekler et al.'s 273-person study, **points, levels and leaderboards each increased the number of outputs but changed neither intrinsic motivation nor perceived competence**, functioning "only for promoting performance quantity" ([Mekler et al. 2017](https://glassmanlab.seas.harvard.edu/annotated_works/mekler17.pdf)). Hanus and Fox's semester-long course comparison found the badge-and-leaderboard section ended with **lower intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and exam scores**, with intrinsic motivation mediating the grade drop ([review table](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0042-5/tables/13)). The novelty curve is also documented: in a 756-student semester the gamification effect began declining after about **four weeks** and stayed depressed for two to six weeks ([Rodrigues et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6)). The only guitar-specific trial, a Huddersfield MSc that added leaderboards to a browser tutor, saw usage time rise from control to personal to group competition but with overlapping confidence intervals, and it never measured playing skill ([Grigutis 2018](https://eprints.hud.ac.uk/id/eprint/34633/1/Laurynas%20Grigutis%20FINAL%20THESIS.PDF)).

The mechanics that do move retention are the ones that encode good practice. Duke, Simmons and Cash had advanced pianists learn a three-bar passage and retested them the next day: **next-day quality correlated with the proportion of error-free run-throughs (r = .71) and with the number of practice mistakes, while total time and number of repetitions predicted nothing** ([Structural Learning briefing on Duke et al. 2009](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/practise-the-hard-bit)). Interleaving produces the familiar learning-performance dissociation: Carter and Grahn's clarinetists scored numerically higher the next day in every comparison after alternating pieces in 3-minute blocks, yet **six of ten still preferred blocked practice** because it felt better in the moment ([Carter & Grahn 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full)). Distributing practice across days improves acquisition and retention of motor skills, with the benefit stronger for continuous skills ([Donovan & Radosevich 1999 meta-analysis](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/1999-donovan.pdf)). And OPTIMAL theory treats learner autonomy as a *learning* variable, not just a motivational one: letting learners choose produced better retention of a motor sequence, a result that replicated for autonomy support while the companion "enhanced expectancies" manipulation did not ([Wulf & Lewthwaite 2016](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/kns_fac_articles/123); [autonomy support study](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/kns_fac_articles/191/)).

### What a mastery meter should meter

Yousician's stars reward consecutive in-time notes across a full run-through, with a multiplier up to 5x, and reviewers note the score "says little about tone, dynamics, feel" ([Yousician support](https://support.yousician.com/hc/en-us/articles/201558362-Practice-vs-Play-mode-Guitar-); [Guitar World](https://www.guitarworld.com/artists/yousician-fast-fun-way-learn-play-guitar-video)). Duke's result suggests a better-aligned meter: count clean repetitions of the *isolated hard spot*, not song-level stars. Rocksmith+ offers a useful compound metric, **mastery = difficulty x accuracy, so 50% difficulty at 90% accuracy reads as 45** ([Ubisoft dev diary](https://www.ubisoft.com/en-ca/game/rocksmith/plus/news-updates/2rN7QW1RC4E4NsipNX1cz7/rocksmith-dev-diary-september-2021)); for a drill trainer the analogue is tempo-fraction x accuracy, so a clean run at 60% speed is never confused with a sloppy run at full speed. Because interleaving and spacing look *worse* in the session, a scheduler that reacts only to in-session accuracy will steer learners toward the blocked, massed practice they already prefer; the scheduler should be allowed to impose interleaving and day-spacing, and the meter should advance only when a passage is clean across spaced sessions rather than within one sitting.

### Tempo ramps: aim for 85% accuracy, step 5%, fall back to the last clean tempo

No published rule like "slow 10% after two misses" exists. Shipping systems adapt on accuracy alone and keep their thresholds secret: Yousician's auto-tempo moves in **5% steps between 25% and 125%** of song speed ([Yousician update 4.14](https://yousician.com/blog/whats-new-in-yousician-update-4-14)), and Rocksmith's dynamic difficulty adds notes per phrase as the player "consistently completes" it and "levels down" after many misses across roughly 20-30 levels ([Wikipedia: Rocksmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocksmith); [The Riff Repeater](https://theriffrepeater.com/?p=12760)). Players complained about mid-song difficulty spikes and many disabled it in favour of looping a riff at reduced speed ([GameSpot review](https://gamespot.com/reviews/rocksmith-2014-edition-review/1900-6415495/)). The closest quantified target is Wilson et al.'s modelling result that **learning rate peaks at about 85% training accuracy (15.87% error)** for simple binary tasks ([Wilson et al. 2019 record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcom/v10y2019i1d10.1038_s41467-019-12552-4.html)), and a 221-person experiment found performance-based difficulty adjustment improved scores without raising felt flow or enjoyment ([Acta Ludologica 2022](https://actaludologica.com/performance-based-dynamic-difficulty-adjustment-and-player-experience-in-a-2d-digital-game-a-controlled-experiment/)). Practitioner convention is to raise the metronome by **2-5 BPM** only once a passage is under control and to drop back to the last clean tempo on failure ([Soundbrenner](https://soundbrenner.com/blog/rhythm-timing-metronome-exercises-for-guitar-part-3); [Yamaha educator tips](https://hub.yamaha.com/music-educators/prof-dev/teaching-tips/practice-effectively/)). A defensible synthesis: target 80-90% note accuracy in the current window, step tempo one 5% notch (3-6 BPM at 60-120 BPM) after a clean window, and on failure step back to the last clean tempo rather than stopping, since retention tracks the proportion of clean attempts. Offer the slow-down as a choice or a setting (Rocksmith exposes separate level-up and level-down speeds and a "Level Up Only" option, [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/221680/discussions/0/143387886723449164)), because autonomy support is itself a retention variable.

### Rewards that inform rather than control

The over-justification literature is the reason to keep the extrinsic layer thin. Deci, Koestner and Ryan's 128-study meta-analysis found **expected tangible rewards reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.28 to -0.40 by contingency), while unexpected rewards and verbal informational feedback did not** ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)). In music students, need satisfaction and autonomous motivation predict more frequent practice, more *quality* practice and a preference for challenge ([Bonneville-Roussy & Evans 2024](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2024_Bonneville-RoussyEvans_MusicStudents.pdf)). The design distinction is framing: a star that *informs* ("clean 4 of 5 at 80 BPM") supports competence; the same star as the *reason* to practise ("earn 3 stars to unlock") is a controlling, expected reward. Leaderboards are the highest-risk element for a solitary practice app, since Hanus and Fox found harm and Sailer and Homner found competition helped only when paired with collaboration.

## Per-note feedback should fade, filter and favour good trials

The guidance hypothesis is the central finding for a trainer that judges every note. Winstein and Schmidt showed that **practice with 50% knowledge of results improved retention over 100% KR even though the 50% group performed worse during practice** ([Reid & Bolton Swafford 2020, summarising Winstein & Schmidt 1990](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7205416)). Summary KR after blocks of 5-15 trials depressed acquisition but improved delayed retention in an inverse relation to summary length ([DTIC report on Schmidt et al. 1989](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA191559.pdf)); bandwidth KR, given only when error leaves a tolerance band, produced higher movement consistency ([Sherwood 1988](https://vivo-cub.colorado.edu/display/pubid_105370)); and learners who chose which trials got feedback learned better under decreased frequency ([Patterson & Carter 2010](https://raw.github.com/cartermaclab/carterlab-papers/main/2010_patterson-carter_learner-regulated-knowledge-of-results-during-the-acquisition-of-multiple-timing-goals.pdf)). The most actionable twist is motivational: when all learners got feedback on 3 of 6 throws, **the group told about its three best trials retained better**, and a child replication also raised intrinsic motivation ([Chiviacowsky & Wulf 2007](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4012854); [RBEFE 2011](https://www.scielo.br/j/rbefe/a/3wjqFcFJZ3rF8SqhLR3yjHj/)). Self-controlled schedules turn out to be performance-dependent: learners ask for feedback after trials they think went well ([Frontiers in Psychology 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01325/full)).

The honest caveat is that this literature comes from simple, discrete, visually fed-back lab tasks. Fujii, Lulic and Chen trained a novel coordination pattern with a tone whose intensity scaled with error and found **the 100% feedback group retained better than the 50% group (p < .05, eta-squared 0.21)**, concluding the guidance hypothesis "was not supported" for a complex auditory task ([Frontiers in Neuroscience 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2016.00251/full)). Sigrist's review frames dependency as real but task- and modality-dependent, and sonification work reports no performance decline when auditory feedback is removed, unlike visual ([Sigrist et al. 2013](https://www.doi.org/10.3758/S13423-012-0333-8); [sonification review](https://scalda.mijn.bsl.nl/transposing-musical-skill-sonification-of-movement-as-concurrent/10207924)). A 15-week singing study of low-frequency feedback came out mixed (3 of 8 retained more under high-frequency phases, 5 less) ([Maxfield 2011](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/doctoral/Application-of-principles-from-motor-learning-theory/9983776919202771)). Playing to a click is continuous and auditory, so the retention penalty for per-note cues is uncertain and probably small; the right response is not to remove per-note feedback but to make dependence cheap to shed.

Two further findings shape how feedback should look in flow. In Pardue and McPherson's violin study, a bar whose position, colour and height encoded the pitch error produced no measurable intonation gain, students said the display **"flickered and changed too fast to act on", and gaze data showed they looked at it only 16% of the time** ([Frontiers in Psychology 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00627/full)). And skilled musicians playing under an external focus (the sound, the audience) earned higher expert ratings for expression and precision than under an internal focus ([Mornell & Wulf 2018](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/kns_fac_articles/224)), which argues for phrasing feedback in terms of the outcome ("late", "muted") rather than the body.

The design that fits all of this: treat timing as bandwidth KR, so a note inside a tolerance band is simply "in time" and only outliers get an early/late cue, with the band tightening as accuracy rises; keep a per-bar or per-run summary even when per-note cues are hidden; offer a "listen only" mode where the app reports only at the end; let the learner request detail after a run; and open the end screen with the best bar and the longest in-time run before the error list. For live counters, render digits with tabular numerals (`tnum`), which removed counter jitter in a 0-to-5 demo and is recommended for dynamically changing content ([Go Make Things](https://gomakethings.com/preventing-layout-shift-with-numbers-using-css/); [Dialtone](https://dialtone.dialpad.com/utilities/typography/font-variant-numeric.html)); Barlow Condensed must be checked for the feature, since many Google Fonts builds lack it ([Dr. Web](https://www.drweb.de/css-ziffern-font-variant-numeric/)). Show at most one derived live number, damp or hide notes-per-minute, and put the per-note judgement at the note's position so the eye never leaves the notation. Monkeytype is the proof that a skill tool can run with almost no in-run reinforcement and a data-rich end screen (WPM, raw, accuracy, consistency as coefficient of variation, a graph) and still hold a community ([monkeytype.com/about](https://monkeytype.com/about)); Duolingo's "gamified moves counter" was "completely neutral" for retention, a warning that another live number is not free ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth)).

## Streaks earn usage, not learning, so make them forgiving

Every streak number worth citing is Duolingo's own A/B result. Decoupling the streak from the daily XP goal (one lesson now extends it) lifted **Day-14 retention +3.3%, DAU +1%, learners on a streak after 20 days +10.5% and new learners on a streak +19%**, and a year later just over half of daily learners held a 7+ day streak, up from about a third; the post is explicit that the metric is usage, not learning, and that fewer learners hit their daily goals afterwards ([Duolingo, Improving the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/)). Two equipped Streak Freezes raised daily actives **+0.38%** without more days off ([Duolingo, How the streak builds habit](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)); the Streak Wager raised Day-7 retention **+14%**; and "learners who binge on Duolingo lessons were much more likely to abandon the app" ([Duolingo, How streaks keep learners committed](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/)). Duolingo's growth lead concedes the early "10-day streak reduces drop-off" finding was largely "correlation and selection bias" ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth)).

The peer-reviewed side is about the cost. Silverman et al. (JCR 2023) found users often treat extending the streak as more important than the activity, **users who break a streak are more likely to leave the platform entirely, and knowing a repair exists lowers motivation to keep the streak** ([The Decision Lab summary](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification)); the same source summarises Etkin's finding that quantification drains enjoyment. A CHI 2016 study of the habit app Lift found streaks create dependency, with users who abandoned the app often dropping the behaviour too ([UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1477627/1/Chi%202016%20LBW%202.1%20camera%20ready.pdf)). Against that, Lally's 96-person habit study reports a median **66 days to peak automaticity** and that "missing one opportunity to perform the behavior did not materially affect the habit formation process" ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit)), so a UI that punishes a single missed day is penalising something that costs nothing in habit terms. For a tool whose goal is skill rather than daily actives, the evidence favours counting any short drill as a practised day, auto-applying one or two quiet freezes rather than selling an "undo" the learner plans around, showing "days practised this month" beside the chain so a break does not zero the record, and framing reminders on the learner's own tempo goal rather than on the count.

Schedule type matters too. Hopson's primer explains that variable-ratio schedules give the steadiest response, fixed-ratio schedules produce post-reward pauses that grow with the ratio, and **"sudden loss of reward is very aversive"; "violation of expectations is perceived as an aggressive act"** ([Game Developer, Behavioral Game Design](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behavioral-game-design)). Harris's critique is that variable reward is the slot-machine mechanism and should be made predictable ([Mobile Ecosystem Forum](https://mobileecosystemforum.com/2017/04/27/slot-machines-in-our-pockets-are-apps-too-good-for-our-own-good/)). A drill trainer needs no artificial unpredictability: the learner's own accuracy already supplies the "will I get it this time" tension, so stars and tempo badges belong at known thresholds, with small tempo steps so the next attainable reward is always close and no growing ratio requirement. Rhythm-game combos that reset on a miss are a tiered fixed-ratio schedule with "one slip wipes it" frustration; Rhythia's variant, where only Perfects feed the multiplier but Goods still count, is the gentler form ([Rhythia mechanics](https://shapes.inc/fandom/rhythia/gameplay-mechanics)).

Celebrations should follow a proportional, stable ladder. Microinteraction guidance holds that feedback should last **200-500 ms and that "users should barely notice a microinteraction unless they're looking for it"**, with prolonged animation becoming annoying on repetitive actions ([Cieden](https://cieden.com/book/sub-atomic/microinteractions/what-are-microinteractions)); Apple's HIG adds that a succinct animation precisely tied to a successful action conveys more than a prominent one and that motion should be avoided on frequent interactions ([Apple HIG Motion](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion)). The ladder that follows: a note gets a 100-200 ms colour or tick change at most (and nothing inside the tolerance band); a clean bar gets a small silent marker; a cleared exercise at a tempo is the one real moment, with the number that improved and a best-ever comparison; the first clean run at target tempo gets the rarest, largest effect; the streak tick lives on the end screen, not in a separate interruption. Attenuate repeats to a quieter version but never remove a tier the learner has already seen, which is Hopson's extinction warning in UI form.

## Luminance, not hue, keeps a long session comfortable

Hue preference is well characterised but only within a culture. Palmer and Schloss found average preference peaks at blue and troughs at chartreuse, with **dark orange (brown) and dark yellow (olive) significantly less liked than other oranges and yellows, saturated colors preferred to light and muted ones**, and their ecological-valence model explaining 80% of variance ([Palmer & Schloss 2010](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/pdf/Palmer%26Schloss%282010%29.pdf)); Himba participants showed "none of the so-called 'universal' patterns" ([Taylor, Clifford & Franklin 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23148465/)). The claim that red impairs performance should not drive design: Gnambs's meta-analysis of 67 effect sizes found **"no evidential value for an effect of red color on intellectual performance"** after publication-bias correction ([Gnambs 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-020-01772-1)), and three direct replications found nothing ([Steele et al. 2017 poster](https://www.appstate.edu/~steelekm/documents/Psychonomic2017Poster_Steele.pdf)). What does replicate is polarity: dark text on a light background gave better acuity and proofreading for young and old adults, with the advantage growing as font size shrank, and fatigue measures showed no polarity effect; at night light mode was faster and small text much harder in dark mode ([NN/g, Dark Mode vs. Light Mode](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/)). NN/g's recommendation is to offer both, follow the OS setting and not force dark mode on everyone. Evening tints barely matter: a warm Night Shift cut melatonin suppression only from 23% to 12%, and the authors conclude brightness must be reduced ([Edison Report on the LRC study](https://edisonreport.com/?p=8678)), so a dim control is worth more than a sepia filter.

For the light page, the strongest study on background colour is Rello and Bigham's 341-reader experiment: **warm low-chroma backgrounds (Peach #EDD1B0, then Orange, then Yellow) gave the shortest reading times and cool pale blues the longest (Blue Grey #DBE1F1 significantly longest, p < .001)** for dyslexic and control readers alike ([Rello & Bigham 2017](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)), and dyslexia guidance independently asks for dark-not-black text on cream or off-white rather than pure white ([Dyslexia Scotland](https://dyslexiascotland.org.uk/contrasting-advice-what-colours-are-best-for-accessibility/)). A cream notation page costs nothing in contrast: computed in the research notes, black on #FFFFF0 is 20.8:1 (Lc 105) and #1A1A1A on #FAF3E0 is 15.7:1 (Lc 97). Keep the tint near-neutral so that amber "off" marks keep their hue distance from the page. Sheet-music apps confirm the convention: Soundslice ships dark mode off by default because light-on-dark notation is "just nontraditional enough" to require opt-in ([Soundslice blog](https://www.soundslice.com/blog/239/introducing-our-players-dark-mode)), and MuseScore 4 keeps the score page white in dark mode while users ask for inversion ([MuseScore issue 340122](https://musescore.org/en/node/340122)).

### Dark theme: WCAG ratios overstate contrast, so use APCA numbers

Material's dark-theme guidance sets the base surface at **#121212, text at 87/60/38% white, elevation by white overlays, and warns that saturated colors "vibrate" and pure white "bleeds" on dark** ([Google dark-theme codelab](https://codelabs.developers.google.com/codelabs/design-material-darktheme)). APCA, which is polarity-aware, reports much lower contrast for dark colors than WCAG 2 and sets **Lc 90 preferred and Lc 75 minimum for body text, Lc 60 for 24px+, Lc 45 for 36px+ or sub-fluent text, Lc 30 as the spot-text floor** ([Myndex APCA introduction](https://github.com/Myndex/apca-introduction)). The practical consequence, computed in the notes, is that Material's 60% white secondary text (#9E9E9E on #121212) passes WCAG AA at 7.0:1 but reaches only Lc -49, below both APCA body-text levels; anything the learner reads repeatedly (tempo, section name) should sit at about #B0B0B0-#BDBDBD or lighter, with 60% white reserved for captions. The table below collects the pairs most useful for the app.

| Pair (text on surface) | WCAG 2 | APCA Lc |
|---|---|---|
| #DEDEDE on #121212 (87% white) | 13.9 | -86 |
| #9E9E9E on #121212 (60% white) | 7.0 | -49 |
| #E7E5E4 on warm dark #1C1917 | 13.9 | -90 |
| #A8A29E on #1C1917 | 6.9 | -51 |
| M3 error #F2B8B5 on #121212 | 11.0 | -72 |
| Okabe-Ito orange #E69F00 on #121212 | 8.3 | -58 |
| Okabe-Ito sky blue #56B4E9 on #121212 | 8.1 | -56 |
| Okabe-Ito blue #0072B2 on #121212 | 3.6 | -26 |
| Okabe-Ito blue #0072B2 on #FFFFFF | 5.2 | 75 |
| #1A1A1A on cream #FAF3E0 | 15.7 | 97 |

A warm near-black such as #1C1917 is the dark analogue of the cream page and matches the Calm extraction's navy-not-grey "pleasant dark" ([webdesignhot Calm tokens](https://www.webdesignhot.com/design.md/calm-app/)), though that palette is an unofficial extraction. For the current-note highlight on a dark tab, WCAG 1.4.11 requires the highlight fill to reach 3:1 against the page and the digit 4.5:1 against the fill ([W3C Understanding SC 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast)); sky blue #56B4E9 or Okabe-Ito yellow #F0E442 with #121212 digits satisfy both, while #0072B2 is too dark for dark pages and ideal on light ones. Okabe-Ito and Tol were designed for light backgrounds, so the dark theme should swap their dark members for Tol's light scheme (#77AADD, #EE8866, #44BB99), which Tol describes as "reasonably distinct in both normal and colour-blind vision" ([Paul Tol's colour schemes](https://sronpersonalpages.nl/~pault/)).

### Colour-blind safe states: orange against blue, never green against red

About **8% of men and 0.4-0.5% of women** have a red-green deficiency, deuteranomaly being by far the most common ([color-blindness.com](https://www.color-blindness.com/50-facts-about-color-blindness/)). The traffic-light convention is the one that collapses: under deutan simulation (Machado 2009 matrices, computed in the notes) a naive #22C55E green versus #EF4444 red drops to ΔE 13 and GitHub's dark-theme green/red pair to ΔE 8, effectively indistinguishable for small marks. Sets whose states differ in *lightness* survive every simulation at ΔE 32 or better.

| Four-state set (correct / early-late / wrong / target) | normal min ΔE | protan | deutan | tritan |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive traffic light #22C55E / #EAB308 / #EF4444 / #3B82F6 | 72 | 25 | 13 | 27 |
| GitHub-dark #3FB950 / #D29922 / #F85149 / #58A6FF | 57 | 16 | 8 | 18 |
| Okabe-Ito #009E73 / #E69F00 / #D55E00 / #56B4E9 | 33 | 25 | 18 | 16 |
| Two-tone + neutrals #FFFFFF / #9E9E9E / #E69F00 / #56B4E9 | 35 | 32 | 35 | 35 |

The recommended encoding follows from the table and from WCAG 1.4.1, which forbids colour as the only carrier of information. The current target is sky blue with an outline ring and a pulse; a correct note simply turns neutral white and stays, so lightness alone carries success; early or late reuse the same neutral at reduced opacity plus a left/right tick; wrong is orange (#E69F00 on dark, #D55E00 on light) plus an X glyph or thicker stroke. If a red is wanted by convention, pair it with blue, never green, and keep it light on dark surfaces (Material 3's #F2B8B5 at 11:1). Rocksmith+ is the direct precedent for the mechanisms to ship: **per-deficiency colour presets, a chroma-normalised "Balanced Colors" preset, a saturation "Color Boost", and a numbers-first "RS Tab" view** ([Ubisoft accessibility spotlight](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/5XfINjoRmVJgBKR8fGt4EM/rocksmith-accessibility-spotlight)); Soundslice adds a global Colors on/off toggle ([Soundslice help](https://soundslice.com/help/en/creating/notations/249/colors)).

### A hue budget of about six, and finger colours that fade

Visualization practice caps effective category hues at **6-12** ([arXiv 1405.5263 citing Ware](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1405.5263)), visual working memory holds about four items and people fall back on coarse colour categories under load ([PMC9539610](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9539610/)). Four finger hues plus a target and a wrong state already make six, so a view should colour by finger *or* by string, never both, with state carried on a different channel (fill versus outline, or the note turning neutral). The one controlled music study is a warning about scaffolds: Rogers' 92 beginning wind players sight-read better *with* pitch colours but **"scored significantly lower when sight-reading plain notation"**, apparently having memorised the colours instead of the notation, even though 65% preferred them ([Wikipedia: Colored music notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colored_music_notation)). That converges with the expertise-reversal effect, where guidance that helps novices becomes redundant and harmful as task-specific knowledge grows ([Kalyuga 2007](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)). No shipping app fades display scaffolds automatically (Yousician's five notation views are user-switched, [Yousician notation options](https://support.yousician.com/hc/en-us/articles/208678605-Notation-options-in-piano)), which makes this an open design space: keep full cues while accuracy is below the target band, remove one layer at a time (colour, then finger number, then fret label) once a passage is clean across spaced sessions, show plain tab in review modes, and let the learner re-enable a cue.

## Flag a miss locally, directionally and in amber, never with a flash

What the research actually warns against is not telling someone they missed; it is attribution and uncontrollability. Learned-helplessness deficits come from exposure to *uncontrollable* outcomes, and failure feedback alone was not associated with poorer performance ([Winefield et al.](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/learned-helplessness-deficits-uncontrollable-outcomes-or-perceive/)). Juul's player research found **"the primary issue is not the frequency of failures, but how failure is communicated"** ([Juul, GDC 2009](https://www.jesperjuul.net/ludologist/?p=590)), and the combination that breeds helplessness is feedback read as internal, stable and global ("it's you, it won't change, you're bad at everything") ([OSU blog applying Juul](https://blogs.oregonstate.edu/inspire/?p=3275)). A specific, fixable cue ("this note, 20 cents sharp") is informative; a global verdict ("Failed", big red X, streak lost) is the risky kind. Gushing in the other direction also backfires: Gardner and Wood found **positively framed errors ("learning opportunities") produced worse performance for high-self-efficacy people** ([Massey record](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/items/e7779b6c-4756-4112-acf1-c2ca9661e564/full)), and the growth-mindset intervention effect is very small across 43 studies ([Sisk et al. 2018](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/sisk-et-al-2018-to-what-extent-and-under-which-circumstances-are-growth-mind-sets-important-to-academic-achievement-two.pdf)). Neutral, directional copy is the safe middle. NN/g's rules supply the form: place the message next to the element, use bold high-contrast text "but never rely on color or animation alone", match treatment to severity (inline for minor, modal only for severe), avoid "invalid", "wrong", "incorrect", avoid humour that goes stale on repetition, and remember that negativity bias and the peak-end rule make the session's worst moment and its last moment disproportionately memorable ([NN/g Error-Message Guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/error-message-guidelines/)).

### Pitch: a damped needle, a +-5 cent dead zone, words that name the fix

Tuners use three metaphors: a needle (centre in tune, left flat, right sharp), a strobe whose pattern drifts left when flat and right when sharp at a speed proportional to error, and an LED ladder with a green centre and red or amber sides ([Peterson StroboSoft FAQ](https://www.petersontuners.com/support/stroboSoft); [Thomann tuner guide](https://www.thomannmusic.com/onlineexpert_page_tuners_display_types.html)). GarageBand is the mainstream precedent for not using red: its needle turns **orange, not red, when flat or sharp** ([GarageBand help](https://help.apple.com/garageband/mac/10.1.5/en.lproj/gbndb40183f2.html)). Hardware precision (PolyTune needle +-0.5 cent, strobe +-0.1, [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/tc-electronic-polytune)) is far finer than perception: the just-noticeable difference is about 3 cents for pure tones, 5-6 cents is the commonly cited human limit, and timbre changes made student musicians miss +-12 cent errors ([arXiv auditory review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.00084); [Wikipedia: Cent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cent_(music))). Since no consumer app publishes its in-tune window, a defensible default is a **green dead zone of about +-5 cents, amber from +-5 to about +-15-20, and the far colour beyond**, with hysteresis and damping so the readout does not flicker (the Pardue and McPherson complaint), note names always visible (their students often lacked them), and the direction spelled out as the physical action ("sharp, tune down"; "flat, tune up"), because consumer guides have to explain which way the needle means to turn the peg.

### Timing: tight windows, single directional words, calibrated latency

Rhythm games show the flow-safe pattern: a tiny transient directional cue near the hit line, never a modal or screen-wide effect. osu!mania's windows are **PERFECT +-16 ms, GREAT 64 minus 3 x OD ms, down to MISS beyond 188 minus 3 x OD ms**, centred on zero with early and late regions on each side ([osu!mania judgement](https://osu.ppy.sh/wiki/en/Gameplay/Judgement/osu!mania)); StepManiaX shows an EARLY or LATE indicator on any judgement below top grade ([Wikipedia: StepManiaX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/StepManiaX)); Project DIVA tilts the judgement left for early and right for late ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1761390/discussions/0/3422185980116444483)). The criticised designs are the ones that give a binary miss with no direction: osu! standard by default, Rhythm Doctor ("judgment is too strict"), and Rocksmith, which "doesn't really outright tell you about your timing" and shows "MISSED" far more than "LATE" ([osu! forum](https://osu.ppy.sh/community/forums/posts/2940941); [Vaporlens](https://vaporlens.app/app/774181/rhythm_doctor); [Rocksmith Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/221680/discussions/0/541906989393368380)). For the drill trainer: a tight "in time" band around +-16-20 ms that gets no marker at all, graded bands out to about +-150 ms labelled only "early" or "late" (lowercase, small, fading in 300-500 ms), "missed" rather than "wrong" beyond the widest window, and a cumulative hit-error bar for the glance. Latency calibration is a prerequisite, since Clone Hero and others expose an audio offset precisely because without it the app blames the player for its own delay ([Clone Hero calibration](https://wiki.clonehero.net/books/guides-and-tutorials/page/calibrating-audio-and-video)), and Yousician's App Store complaints about notes "turning red" on target read like exactly that failure ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/yousician-learn-play-guitar/id959883039)).

### A lowered tempo: forewarn when possible, otherwise a 3-5 s chip with one action

Duolingo's "hard exercise" indicator is the best-documented precedent for announcing a difficulty change: a small message at the top of the screen before a harder item, after which learners spent more time and returned more the next day, and one said **"If I make a mistake, I don't feel as bad, because you warned me that it was hard. If I get it right, I feel proud."** ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-difficult-exercises/)). Rocksmith's level-down notices show "for only a few seconds" ([The Riff Repeater](https://theriffrepeater.com/?p=12760)), and platform guidance puts this class of notice in a snackbar or toast (short, auto-dismiss, one at a time), with third-party figures of about 4 s short and 10 s long and a slightly longer run when the snackbar carries an action ([ProAndroidDev](https://proandroiddev.com/how-to-set-custom-duration-for-material3-snackbar-in-jetpack-compose-497fbc491f8b)); a banner that must be dismissed is for "microphone not detected", not for a per-run tempo change. The pattern: a transient chip near the tempo readout reading "Tempo 80 to 72" with a single action "Keep 80", auto-dismissing in 3-5 s with a fade, the persistent state carried only by the tempo number and a small "auto" tag, and a setting that decides whether the app may slow the learner at all (Rocksmith's "Level Up Only"). Where the exercise structure allows it, forewarn ("faster part next") rather than announce after misses.

### Motion, haptics and copy

Apple's HIG asks for motion that is brief, precise and optional, warns that "gratuitous or excessive animation can distract people and may make them feel disconnected or physically uncomfortable", and says to avoid adding motion to frequent interactions ([Apple HIG Motion](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion)); with Reduce Motion on, the replacements are "a dissolve, highlight fade, or color shift" ([Reduced Motion criteria](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-app-accessibility/reduced-motion-evaluation-criteria)). WCAG 2.3.1 caps flashing at **three per second with a stricter red-flash threshold** ([wcag.dock.codes](https://wcag.dock.codes/documentation/wcag232)). So a miss cue is a 100-200 ms colour shift or small shape change at the note's position, with no shake and no full-screen flash, and a reduce-motion variant that is a pure opacity change. Haptics follow a documented vocabulary: Apple's four-pulse "Error" is noticeably harsher than two-pulse "Warning", a tuning or timing miss is at most warning-class, and "the best haptic experience is one that people may not be conscious of, but miss when it's turned off"; Apple also warns that vibration can disrupt the microphone, so haptics should be tested or default off while recording ([Apple HIG Playing haptics](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-haptics)); Android offers `REJECT` and `CONFIRM` constants on API 30+ ([HapticFeedbackConstants](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/view/HapticFeedbackConstants)). Copy is one or two words in flow ("sharp", "flat", "early", "late"), the physical fix where there is room, magnitude in cents only for players who opt in, and the growth framing reserved for the session summary ("Not yet at 100 bpm: 3 of 4 bars clean") rather than the live display; avoid "wrong", "invalid", "fail", exclamation marks on misses and jokes. None of this microcopy has been tested in a practice app; it is assembled from the guidelines above.

## Conclusion

The four questions share one answer: the app should spend its design effort on *what it measures and when it speaks*, not on how loudly it rewards. A meter fed by clean repetitions of the hard spot, advanced only across spaced sessions, a tempo ramp that targets roughly 85% accuracy in 5% steps and retreats to the last clean tempo, and feedback that stays silent inside a tolerance band and leads with the learner's best bar together encode the only mechanics with retention evidence, and they do so without a single badge. Streaks, stars and celebrations are a thin layer on top whose job is to keep the learner coming back during the first weeks of habit formation and then get out of the way; the evidence on dependency and broken streaks says they should be forgiving, predictable and quiet rather than loss-averse.

The most useful reframing for the visual layer is that comfort and gentleness are both mostly luminance problems. A cream page and a warm near-black surface, text at APCA rather than WCAG levels, and state colours that differ in lightness (neutral for correct, dimmer neutral for early or late, orange for wrong, blue for the target) solve long-session fatigue, colour-blind safety and non-aggressive error signalling with one palette decision. What remains unmeasured is specific to this app and worth instrumenting: whether per-note timing cues on a guitar drill actually cost retention (the lab literature says yes, the auditory-task literature says no), at what accuracy threshold finger colours should fade, and how learners respond to a transient tempo-drop chip versus a forewarning. Those three are cheap A/B tests inside a trainer that already logs every note.
